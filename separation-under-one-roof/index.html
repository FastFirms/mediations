Separated under one roof in Australia

Many couples separate while still living in the same home, for financial reasons, for the children, or while they sort out next steps. It is legal, it is common, and handled well it can be calm. This guide explains how separation under one roof is recognised, the evidence you need, the ground rules that keep the peace, and how to move forward without court.

What is separation under one roof?

"Separation under one roof" describes a couple who have decided their relationship is over but continue, at least for a time, to live in the same home. It is common and entirely valid. People stay under one roof for financial reasons — running two households is expensive — for the stability of the children, while a property settlement is sorted out, or simply because finding somewhere else to live takes time.

What matters legally is not whether you share an address, but whether the relationship has genuinely ended. In Australia you can be separated for every legal purpose — divorce, property settlement, Centrelink — while still sleeping under the same roof, provided you can show the marriage or de facto relationship is over in substance.

Why so many couples stay under one roof

With rents and mortgage repayments where they are, running two households straight away is out of reach for many Australian families. Staying in the same home for a while is often the sensible, responsible choice, not a sign that the separation is not real.

The most common reasons we hear are:

  • Money. One income cannot cover a mortgage plus rent elsewhere, at least until a property settlement is reached.
  • The children. Parents want to keep routines stable, especially through exams or the school year, and plan the move carefully.
  • Protecting your position. Some people worry that moving out will weaken their claim to the home. It does not, but the worry is understandable.
  • Time. Finding a rental, sorting finances and deciding who keeps the house all take longer than people expect.

Staying put can work well for months, but only if it is managed deliberately. Without clear agreements, tension builds, small issues become big ones, and the process that could have been settled calmly ends up in front of lawyers and judges.

Is separation under one roof legally recognised in Australia?

Yes. Under the Family Law Act 1975, separation happens when at least one person forms the intention to end the relationship, communicates that to the other, and acts on it. Nothing in the law requires you to live at different addresses. The courts have long accepted that a couple can be "separated but living under the same roof", and the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia has a specific process for proving it when you apply for a divorce.

This matters because a divorce in Australia requires you to be separated for at least 12 months and one day before you can apply. Time spent separated under one roof counts towards that 12 months — you just need to be able to evidence it.

How do you show you've genuinely separated?

Because you're still living together, you may need to demonstrate that the relationship has actually ended rather than merely become strained. No single factor is decisive; a court or agency looks at the overall picture across several areas of life.

You don't need every box ticked. The point is to show a consistent pattern that the relationship has ended, not just a rough patch. Keeping a brief note of when things changed — when you moved to a separate room, when you split the accounts — makes this far easier to evidence later.

The date of separation matters

Your date of separation sets the clock running on time limits for both divorce and property. It can also affect Centrelink entitlements and how assets are valued. If you're separated under one roof, pin the date down early so you understand exactly where you stand.

Divorce: the affidavit evidence the Court needs

If you lived under one roof during the 12-month separation period, the Federal Circuit and Family Court requires you to support your divorce application with an affidavit: a written statement sworn or affirmed before an authorised witness such as a lawyer or Justice of the Peace.

Sole vs joint applications

What the affidavit needs to explain

The Court asks you to show a change in the marriage, gradual or sudden, by explaining any:

  • change in sleeping arrangements;
  • reduction in shared activities or family outings;
  • decline in performing household duties for each other;
  • division of finances, such as separate bank accounts;
  • other matters showing the marriage has broken down, such as telling family and friends.

It should also explain why you continued to live in the same home and whether you intend to change that, the arrangements for any children under 18 during that time, and which government departments (such as Centrelink or Child Support) you told about the separation, attaching any correspondence. These are examples of evidence, not a checklist that guarantees an outcome.

How your separation date affects time limits

Separation isn't only about divorce. It also starts the clock on the deadlines for finalising a property settlement, and those deadlines differ for married and de facto couples.

Applying after a deadline isn't impossible, but you need the court's permission, which adds cost and uncertainty. If a deadline is approaching, it is worth formalising arrangements while everyone is still cooperating. Read our property settlement guide →

Centrelink: the official SS293 form

If you receive, or are claiming, a Centrelink payment, Services Australia uses the Relationship details – Separated under one roof form (SS293) to assess whether to pay you as a single person or as a member of a couple. Download it from the official page rather than a copy elsewhere, because forms are updated.

Two forms, and a safety exception

Services Australia says you and your ex-partner each need to complete a form, and your ex-partner returns theirs to you so you can submit both. If it puts you at risk, your ex-partner does not need to complete the form. Do not make unsafe contact simply to get paperwork; tell Services Australia about your situation.

No payment amount is guaranteed

Completing SS293 lets Services Australia make an assessment; it does not guarantee single-rate payments or any particular amount. Ask Services Australia about your eligibility and actual circumstances. Use the same separation date and facts you would give the Court, so your records are consistent.

SS293 is not a divorce form

SS293 is for Centrelink. The Court separately requires affidavit evidence for a divorce where you lived under one roof. One does not satisfy the other.

A house agreement: the ground rules that keep the peace

The single most effective step for couples separated under one roof is a written house agreement. It turns unspoken expectations, which cause most of the conflict, into clear arrangements both of you have signed up to.

A house agreement is usually informal and not court-enforceable, but it is powerful evidence of separation and a practical way to avoid conflict.

Many couples find these conversations hard to have alone, because every topic is loaded. This is exactly where a mediator helps: a neutral person keeps the discussion focused, makes sure both of you are heard, and writes up what you agree so there is no argument later about what was said.

Money and bills while you share a home

Finances are usually the first flashpoint. Separate the money early and keep it simple:

  • Open an account in your own name for your income, and stop pooling money for personal spending.
  • Agree how joint costs are split, often in proportion to income, and write it down.
  • Keep receipts for what you pay. Contributions after separation can be taken into account in the property settlement.
  • Do not move, hide or run down joint assets. Both of you have a duty of full and frank disclosure.

Children: keeping things steady

Children usually notice far more than parents realise. Living under one roof can be a gentle way to manage the transition, as long as the children are protected from adult conflict.

  • Tell them together, in age-appropriate language, and reassure them it is not their fault.
  • Keep routines predictable: the same school runs, bedtimes and activities.
  • Never argue in front of them or ask them to carry messages.
  • Start planning the future arrangement now, so the eventual move is not a shock.

A parenting plan agreed while you are still under one roof gives children certainty about what happens next, and it makes the move itself far smoother. Parenting plan mediation is designed for exactly this.

Living together while separated: practical tips

The arrangement works best when expectations are clear. A few things make a real difference:

  • Agree on space and schedules — who uses common areas when, and how you'll handle meals, laundry and the bathroom.
  • Separate your money early — open individual accounts and divide responsibility for bills so nothing falls through the cracks.
  • Keep the children's routine steady — shield them from conflict and present a united, calm front about the practical changes.
  • Document the change — note your separation date and the adjustments that followed, which helps with both divorce and Centrelink later.
  • Plan the exit — treat the shared-roof period as temporary and agree a rough timeline for one person to move on.

A worked example

An illustrative scenario, not a real client.

Sam and Priya decided to separate in February but could not afford for either of them to move out before the end of the school year. They had two children, a mortgage and a shared credit card.

  1. Week 1: Sam moved into the spare room. They each opened a personal account and told their families.
  2. Week 3: In a single mediation session they agreed a house agreement: mortgage split by income, groceries shared, alternating weekends with the children, no new partners in the home, and a target move date in December.
  3. Months 2 to 9: With the rules written down, arguments dropped away. They both kept short notes of key dates.
  4. Month 10: A second session settled the property division and a parenting plan. Their lawyers formalised it with consent orders.
  5. Month 13: They applied for divorce with affidavits explaining the period under one roof, which the house agreement helped evidence.

No court hearing, no contested litigation, and two parents who could still sit together at school events.

Safety and getting advice

Living with a former partner can become unsafe. If you are in immediate danger, call 000. For confidential support, contact 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732. Mediation may not be appropriate where there is family violence; see domestic violence and family law. For questions about your divorce application or property rights, get independent legal advice.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Can you be separated while living together?+

Yes. 'Separation under one roof' is recognised in Australia under the Family Law Act 1975. You may need to show the relationship has genuinely ended despite living in the same home.

How do you prove separation under one roof?+

Through factors like separate sleeping arrangements, separated finances, reduced shared activities, and informing family, friends and relevant agencies that you've separated. When applying for divorce you'll usually file an affidavit, plus a corroborating affidavit from an independent person.

Does time separated under one roof count towards the 12 months for divorce?+

Yes. A divorce requires 12 months and one day of separation, and time spent separated under the same roof counts — provided you can evidence that the relationship had genuinely ended.

Why does the date of separation matter?+

It can affect time limits for divorce and property applications, Centrelink entitlements, and how assets are valued. Getting clarity on your separation date early helps you understand your legal position.

Can you still claim Centrelink as separated if you live together?+

You may be assessed as single even while sharing an address. Services Australia can recognise separation under one roof and may ask for a separation form and supporting details.

Is SS293 the divorce form?+

No. SS293 helps Services Australia assess whether you are paid as a single person or as a member of a couple. The Court separately requires affidavit evidence where separation under one roof is relevant to a divorce application.

Must my former partner complete SS293?+

Services Australia says each person generally completes a form, but your ex-partner does not need to if doing so puts you at risk. Read the current official instructions and ask Services Australia about your situation.

What evidence might the Court need?+

The Court lists changes in sleeping arrangements, shared activities, household duties and finances, and other evidence the marriage has broken down. It also asks why you stayed in the home and about arrangements for children.

Does separation under one roof guarantee a payment amount?+

No. Services Australia uses the SS293 information to assess single or couple status. Ask Services Australia about eligibility and your actual circumstances; no amount can be promised from separation alone.

Can we mediate while still living together?+

Yes. Mediation can help you agree on living arrangements, finances and parenting while under one roof, and can be conducted in separate rooms or online if being together is difficult.

Should I move out if we separate under one roof?+

Not necessarily. Moving out does not mean giving up your share of the home, but you should agree arrangements for bills, children and property first. Get advice before deciding, and consider mediation to agree a plan.

What is a house agreement?+

A house agreement is a written set of ground rules for separated couples still living together, covering space, money, children, visitors and an end date. It reduces conflict and helps evidence that you have separated.
