Family Law Mediation: What Happens If You Don't Want to Do It?

Dan Toombs Founder & Accredited Mediator · AMDRAS · Multi-Award Winning Lawyer

Key takeaway: You cannot simply refuse to engage with family law mediation and expect no consequences. For parenting disputes, attendance at FDR is a legal prerequisite for court. Refusing earns you a Section 60I certificate that reflects your refusal — and courts take this seriously. For property disputes, mediation is not mandatory but courts expect genuine attempts at negotiation, and persistent refusal can affect costs orders.

Is mediation compulsory in Australian family law?

The answer depends on what type of dispute you have. For parenting disputes under the Family Law Act 1975, attendance at family dispute resolution (FDR) with an accredited practitioner is a legal prerequisite before you can file parenting proceedings in the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA). You must attempt FDR — or obtain a formal exemption — before the court will even accept your application.

For property settlement disputes, there is no mandatory FDR requirement. You can apply to court for property orders without first attempting mediation. However, the court's own case management procedures typically require early dispute resolution steps — and a party who refuses all reasonable offers of negotiation or mediation risks an adverse costs order.

So: refusal has consequences, whether or not mediation is technically mandatory for your dispute type.

What happens if you refuse: parenting matters

If you refuse to attend FDR for a parenting dispute, the accredited FDRP issues a Type 1 Section 60I certificate — certifying that the other party invited you to FDR and you declined. This certificate allows the other party to file parenting proceedings immediately. You have essentially handed them the key to the courthouse door.

More significantly, courts treat non-engagement with FDR seriously. Under the Family Law Act and the court's general powers, a court can make adverse costs orders against a party whose conduct — including refusal to engage with FDR — has unnecessarily prolonged proceedings or increased the other party's costs.

Beyond costs, refusing FDR signals to the court that you are unwilling to co-operate in resolving parenting disputes. In a system where the court is assessing what is in the children's best interests, a track record of obstruction and non-cooperation is not helpful to your case.

What happens if you refuse: property matters

For property settlements, refusing mediation does not by itself generate a certificate or block your court access. However, courts in Australia have a strong culture of encouraging and rewarding parties who make genuine attempts to resolve disputes without judicial intervention. The court's practice directions require parties to comply with pre-action procedures — which include genuine attempts to exchange information and negotiate — before filing.

A party who goes straight to court without any genuine negotiation attempt, or who refuses all reasonable offers of mediation, may find themselves exposed to a costs order when the matter concludes. If the outcome they achieve at trial is no better (or worse) than what was offered in settlement, the court can and does order the unreasonable party to pay the other's costs from the point the reasonable offer was made.

The Section 60I certificate and costs consequences

There are five types of Section 60I certificate. The one that matters most for a party who refuses is Type 4 — issued where one party attended but did not make a genuine effort to resolve the dispute. Type 4 certificates signal to the court that one party went through the motions without genuine engagement.

Unlike Type 1 (where the refusing party doesn't attend at all), Type 4 applies to people who physically show up but use the session to delay, obstruct, or signal bad faith. Courts can take this into account when making costs orders — treating the Type 4 party as though they had unnecessarily prolonged the proceedings.

The message from the legislation is clear: the law wants parties to engage genuinely with FDR. Simply turning up and refusing to consider any compromise does not satisfy the requirement — and can make your legal position worse.

Legitimate reasons not to mediate

There are genuine situations where mediation is not appropriate, and the law recognises them:

  • Family violence: Where there is a history or risk of family violence, mediation may not be safe or fair. The power imbalance created by family violence can prevent genuine participation
  • Active criminal proceedings: Where criminal proceedings are underway relating to the same matter, it may not be appropriate to mediate
  • Significant mental health issues: Where one party cannot participate meaningfully due to mental health or cognitive capacity issues
  • Geographic or logistical barriers: In some cases, practical barriers make attendance impossible — though online mediation now removes most geographic barriers

If any of these apply to your situation, the appropriate course is to seek a formal exemption — not simply to decline and hope there are no consequences.

Formal exemptions from FDR

Section 60I(9) of the Family Law Act provides formal exemptions from the FDR requirement. You may proceed directly to court without a certificate if:

  • A party or child has experienced or is at risk of family violence
  • A child would be at risk if there were a delay
  • The application is urgent
  • A party is unable to participate due to incapacity
  • Existing court orders (other than consent orders) already deal with the matter

These exemptions must be claimed in your court application with an explanation of the basis. The court will assess whether the exemption is genuine.

What if the other party refuses to mediate?

If you are the party trying to initiate FDR and the other party refuses, this works in your favour. The accredited FDRP will issue you a Type 1 certificate confirming that you invited the other party and they declined. You can then proceed to file parenting proceedings. The other party's refusal may be raised with the court and considered when costs are assessed.

For property matters, if the other party refuses all reasonable attempts at negotiation or mediation, document your attempts carefully. This creates a record that demonstrates your good faith and the other party's obstructive behaviour — relevant if costs are argued at the conclusion of proceedings.

Why most people who try mediation are glad they did

In our experience at Mediations Australia, the people who are most resistant to mediation — those who arrive convinced that nothing will be resolved — are often the same people who walk out with an agreement. The structured process, the neutral facilitator, and the absence of the adversarial framework all create conditions where agreement becomes possible when it seemed impossible.

Mediation also gives both parties something court cannot: the chance to hear the other person's perspective without it being weaponised. In our sessions, parties often discover that the distance between their positions is smaller than they imagined — the conflict was partly driven by assumptions about what the other party wanted, rather than what they actually want.

The question "do I want to do mediation?" almost always has a better answer than "do I want to spend the next two years in court?" Choosing court over mediation rarely saves time, money, or emotional energy. It almost always costs more of all three.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be forced to attend mediation?

For parenting matters, you cannot be physically forced to attend. But refusing to attend generates a certificate that allows the other party to file in court, and courts take a dim view of parties who refuse to engage with FDR without a legitimate exemption. In practice, the pressure to attend is significant.

What if I'm scared of the other party?

This is a completely legitimate concern and one our mediators take seriously. We can conduct sessions by shuttle (parties in separate rooms or separate video calls), arrange separate arrival and departure times, and structure the session so there is no direct contact. If the risk is such that even shuttle mediation is unsafe, a family violence exemption may apply — speak to us or a family lawyer before deciding.

What if I think mediation is a waste of time?

Many people who have this view have not experienced well-facilitated mediation. Court proceedings are enormously expensive, slow, and uncertain — and they typically end in a negotiated outcome anyway, just later. If you believe your case is strong enough for court, get legal advice first. In most cases, experienced family lawyers encourage mediation even for strong cases — because trial outcomes are never guaranteed and the costs are real.

Can I attend mediation and then still go to court?

Yes. Attending mediation and reaching a partial agreement does not prevent you from proceeding to court on the remaining unresolved issues. If FDR produces no agreement, the FDRP issues a certificate allowing you to file. Attending FDR is not a commitment to settle — it is a commitment to genuinely explore settlement. The court option remains open if FDR fails.
