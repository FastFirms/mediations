Key takeaway: A Section 60I certificate is a document issued by an accredited Family Dispute Resolution (FDR) practitioner — such as a registered mediator — confirming that you have attended, attempted, or been assessed as exempt from FDR before applying for parenting orders. It is a legal requirement under section 60I of the Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) for most parenting applications filed in the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia. Without the right certificate, the court will not accept your application.

What is a Section 60I FDR certificate?

Under Family Law Act 1975, parties seeking parenting orders must first attempt Family Dispute Resolution — a structured, impartial process where an accredited practitioner helps both parents try to reach an agreement about their children's arrangements. After completing (or attempting) FDR, the practitioner issues a certificate under section 60I(5) of the Act confirming what occurred. That certificate — commonly called a "Section 60I certificate" or "FDR certificate" — is the document you file with your court application.

The purpose of the requirement is straightforward: courts want parents to attempt resolution before consuming court time and resources. Where parents do reach an agreement at FDR, a court application may not be necessary at all — the agreement can be formalised as consent orders or a parenting plan without a judge ever being involved.

The certificate is issued by an Mediator Standards Board (AMDRAS)-accredited FDR practitioner, which is the qualification the government requires of mediators offering this service. When you need a Section 60I certificate

You need a Section 60I certificate when you are filing an application for a parenting order — including applications about:

  • Who a child lives with (what was previously called "residence")
  • The time a child spends with each parent (previously called "contact" or "access")
  • Parental responsibility — who makes major long-term decisions for a child
  • Communication between a child and a parent or other person
  • Any other aspect of the care, welfare, or development of a child

You do not need a Section 60I certificate for purely financial applications (property settlement, spousal maintenance, child support), consent orders, or for applications that fall within the exemptions listed below.

The four types of Section 60I certificate

There are four types of Section 60I certificate, and which one you receive matters — because the certificate tells the court exactly what happened at FDR, and the type can affect how the court views the parties' conduct.

A Type 3 certificate — where a party is found not to have made a genuine effort — carries a consequence: under Family Law Act 1975, the court may take that failure into account when making orders about costs. In practice, this creates a genuine incentive for both parents to engage constructively at FDR rather than simply attending to tick a box.

A Type 4 certificate is the most common outcome when both parties attend and engage but simply cannot bridge their differences at the table. It confirms that FDR was attempted in good faith, and it allows you to file your application with the court if needed.

Exemptions: when you don't need to attend FDR

Section 60I(9) of the Family Law Act 1975 sets out the circumstances in which a person may apply for parenting orders without first attending FDR and without a Section 60I certificate. These exemptions include:

  • Family violence or child abuse: where there has been, or there is a risk of, family violence, or abuse or risk of abuse of a child
  • Urgency: where the matter is urgent
  • Consent orders: where the application is by consent of all parties
  • Contravention: where a court order has been contravened and there are reasonable grounds to believe the contravention was intentional
  • Response applications: where the application is a response to another application already before the court
  • Inability to participate: where a party is unable to participate effectively in FDR (for example, due to incapacity or geographic remoteness that makes participation impractical)

Even where a family violence exemption applies, it is worth speaking with an FDR practitioner early. An experienced practitioner can assess whether shuttle mediation — where the parties never meet face-to-face — or another adapted format might be workable and beneficial, even where direct contact would not be appropriate. See also our guide to domestic violence and family law for more information on safety planning in the family law process.

If you believe a family violence exemption applies to your situation, you should seek legal advice before deciding how to proceed.

How to get a Section 60I certificate

The process for obtaining a Section 60I certificate is straightforward when both parties are willing to engage. Here is a typical pathway:

  1. Contact an accredited FDR provider. The practitioner must hold accreditation under the National Mediator Accreditation System (NMAS) as a Family Dispute Resolution Practitioner — not all mediators hold this credential. Government-funded services are available through Attorney-General's Dept — Family Relationship Centres and the Family Relationships Online, though wait times can be several months. Private FDR practitioners like Mediations Australia typically have shorter wait times and more scheduling flexibility.
  2. Initial assessment. The FDR practitioner will conduct a preliminary assessment — often called a MIAM or intake — to assess both parties' suitability for FDR, screen for family violence or safety concerns, and explain the process.
  3. Attend FDR. Both parties (either together or separately via shuttle mediation) work with the practitioner to try to reach an agreement. Sessions can be held in person or via online mediation.
  4. Certificate issued. Whether or not agreement is reached, the practitioner issues a Section 60I certificate. If one party refuses to attend, the practitioner can issue a certificate after attempting to bring them to the table.

How long is the certificate valid?

There is no statutory expiry date on a Section 60I certificate under the Family Law Act 1975. Once issued, the certificate does not automatically lapse. However, in practice, the passage of significant time between the certificate and a court application may be noted by the court, particularly if circumstances have changed substantially. If your certificate is more than a year old and the matter has not yet been filed, it is worth seeking legal advice on whether a fresh attempt at FDR — and a new certificate — would better reflect the current situation.

A note on Western Australia

Western Australia operates its own separate family court system — the Family Court of Western Australia — under the Family Court Act 1997 (WA). While the FDR requirement for parenting matters applies nationally, the specific procedures and forms differ from the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia. If you are in WA, ensure you are working with an FDR practitioner familiar with WA-specific processes and filing requirements.

After the certificate: what comes next

Once you have your Section 60I certificate, you have three main options:

  1. File for parenting orders using the certificate. This is necessary where agreement has broken down and you need the court to resolve the matter. See our guide to the family court process for what to expect.
  2. Formalise the agreement reached at FDR as consent orders, which are filed with the court but do not require a hearing. This gives the agreement the legal force of a court order.
  3. Document the agreement as a parenting plan — a written record of what was agreed. A parenting plan is not legally enforceable in the same way as orders, but it provides clarity and a shared reference point for both parents. See our parenting plans guide for when this works and when it doesn't.

If you reached agreement at FDR but want it to be binding, consent orders are almost always the better choice. For help understanding which option suits your situation, a free initial consultation with one of our accredited mediators is a good starting point.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a Section 60I certificate without attending FDR?+

Yes, in certain circumstances. If the other party refuses to attend, the FDR practitioner can issue a Type 1 certificate without both parties being present. If the matter is assessed as inappropriate for FDR (for example, due to family violence), a Type 2 certificate can be issued. Various other exemptions also apply — including urgency, consent orders, and contravention of orders.

What if my ex refuses to attend FDR?+

If the other party refuses or fails to attend FDR after being invited by the practitioner, the FDR practitioner will issue a Type 1 Section 60I certificate. This certificate allows you to file your application with the court. You are not stuck — a refusal to participate does not prevent you from proceeding.

Can I get a Section 60I certificate from any mediator?+

No. The practitioner must hold specific accreditation as a Family Dispute Resolution Practitioner under the National Mediator Accreditation System. Not all mediators hold this credential. Always confirm accreditation before booking FDR if you need a Section 60I certificate for a court application.

Does a Type 3 certificate hurt my chances in court?+

A Type 3 certificate — where a party did not make a genuine effort — can be taken into account by the court when making costs orders. It may also reflect negatively on that party's willingness to co-operate. This is why engaging genuinely in FDR matters, even when you expect to disagree.

Do I need a Section 60I certificate for property matters?+

No. The Section 60I certificate requirement applies only to parenting applications, not property settlement, financial orders, or child support. However, some couples choose to address parenting and financial matters together at FDR, even though only the parenting issues require a certificate.

How much does it cost to get a Section 60I certificate?+

Government-funded Family Relationship Centres offer free or low-cost FDR, though wait times can be long. Private FDR providers charge a professional fee that varies by provider, the complexity of the matter, and the number of sessions required. Many providers, including Mediations Australia, will confirm their fee structure upfront.
