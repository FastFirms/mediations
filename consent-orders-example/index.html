Consent orders examples in Australia

Consent orders are court orders made after parties agree on proposed arrangements and the Court approves them. This guide walks through an annotated example layout, two worked property scenarios and a parenting example, then the documents, fee and checks involved. Use it to understand the structure, not as wording to copy.

Key takeaway: A consent orders example shows the layout: a heading, the words "by consent the Court orders", then numbered orders saying who does what and by when, with parenting and property orders kept separate. It cannot tell you which orders suit your family. Property orders must be just and equitable, parenting orders must be in the child's best interests, and the Court decides whether to make them.

What a consent order example can and cannot show

People search for examples because a blank template is daunting. An example can show you how proposed orders are usually organised: who does what, by when, and what happens if a step is not completed. It cannot show you what should be in your orders, because that depends on your assets, debts, superannuation, children and circumstances.

The Court applies different legal tests to the two kinds of orders. According to the Court's guidance on how to FCFCOA: Apply for consent orders, parenting orders must be in the best interests of the child, and financial or property orders must be just and equitable. Agreement between you is the starting point, not the finish line: the Court still has to be satisfied the orders meet those requirements under the Family Law Act 1975.

An application has two parts. The first is the Application for Consent Orders, which gives the Court the information it needs to decide whether to make the orders. The second is the proposed orders themselves: the actual wording you want the Court to make.

An annotated example of proposed orders

Below is a simplified, fictional layout for a couple, Party A and Party B, who want both property and parenting orders. The notes on the right explain what each part is doing.

Example text (fictional)What this part does
Proposed orders. [Party A] and [Party B]. File number [to be allocated].Identifies the parties and the proceeding. The Court's template sets the exact heading.
By consent, the Court orders that:Signals the orders are agreed, not decided after a contested hearing.
Parenting orders
1. The child [Name, born DD/MM/YYYY] live with [Party A].Where the child lives. Each order deals with one obligation.
2. The child spend time with [Party B] each alternate weekend from [time, day] to [time, day], commencing [date].Specific days, times and a start date avoid later arguments about what was meant.
3. Changeover occur at [location].Practical arrangements reduce conflict at handovers.
Property orders
4. Within [X] days, [Party B] transfer to [Party A] all right, title and interest in [property at ADDRESS].A transfer with a deadline. The property must be identified precisely.
5. [Party A] refinance the mortgage over that property to release [Party B] within [X] days, failing which the property be sold.A fallback if a step fails, so the orders do not stall.
6. [Party A] retain the funds in [account] and [Party B] retain the vehicle [registration].Who keeps which asset.
7. [Party B] be solely responsible for the debt owed to [lender] and indemnify [Party A] against it.Allocates debts, not just assets.
8. Each party and the Trustee have liberty to apply in relation to the implementation of the orders affecting the superannuation interest.Required wording where super is split (see below).
9. Except as provided in these orders, each party retain the property in their possession.A catch-all so nothing is left unaddressed.
Signed: [Party A], [Party B]. Date: [DD/MM/YYYY].Both parties sign the proposed orders.

The Court publishes a proposed orders template, and the parenting fact sheet referred to on its consent orders page helps with parenting wording. Real orders are usually longer and more precise than this example, particularly where there is a business, a trust or a superannuation split.

Two worked property scenarios

These fictional scenarios show how the same structure handles different situations. The figures are invented and the outcomes are not recommendations: what is just and equitable depends on contributions, future needs and other factors a lawyer can assess.

Scenario 1: one home, two super funds, one car loan

Item (fictional)ValueHow the orders would need to deal with it
Home, net of mortgage$420,000Transfer to one party with a refinance deadline, or sale and division of proceeds
Party A super$180,000Retain, or split by a superannuation splitting order (trustee notice required)
Party B super$60,000As above
Car loan-$18,000Allocate responsibility and an indemnity
Joint savings$25,000Divide and close the account by a set date

Whatever split the parties agree, the orders translate it into actions: who transfers what, who refinances, whether super is split and by how much, and what happens if a step is missed.

Scenario 2: renting, no real estate, a small business

Party A runs a sole-trader business; there is no home, but there is a vehicle used in the business and a tax debt. Here the orders need to identify the business assets, address the tax liability, and be clear about who keeps the business and any future obligations. A lawyer and, often, an accountant should look at tax consequences before orders are finalised.

Superannuation splitting: the extra steps

Super is the part of a property settlement most often underestimated. The Court's FCFCOA: Apply for consent orders page sets out specific requirements:

  • At least 28 days before filing, written notice of the proposed orders must be served on the trustee of the fund, stating that the trustee may object within 28 days.
  • If the trustee does not object within 28 days of receiving the notice, the application can be filed, with proof of the trustee's response and proof of the current value of the interest.
  • The proposed orders must include a provision that each party and the trustee have liberty to apply about implementing the orders affecting the super interest.
  • Without this proof, the Court cannot make the orders sought.

Legal and, where needed, accounting advice is recommended, including on tax consequences.

Parenting consent orders example

Parenting orders deal with arrangements for children rather than money. There is no automatic starting point of equal time: the question is what arrangement is in the best interests of the particular child. Parenting orders commonly cover:

TopicExample of what the order addresses (fictional)
Living arrangementsThe children live with [Party A]
School-term timeAlternate weekends and one weeknight with [Party B]
School holidaysHalf of each school holiday period, alternating the first half each year
Special daysChristmas, Mother's and Father's Day, birthdays
CommunicationPhone or video calls with the other parent at set times
DecisionsHow major long-term decisions, such as schooling and health, are made
ChangeoversWhere and how handovers happen

Where there are concerns about family violence or child safety, templates are not a substitute for legal advice. Parenting consent applications must include a Notice of child abuse, family violence or risk. See our guide to domestic violence and family law and the parenting plans guide.

Filing: documents, fee and checklist

For the federal Court, consent applications are filed through the Commonwealth Courts Portal. The Court's page lists the documents to upload:

DocumentWhen it is needed
Application for Consent OrdersAlways
Proposed orders (using the Court's template), signedAlways
An identical unsigned Word (.docx) version of the proposed orders, not locked and without tracked changesAlways
Notice of child abuse, family violence or riskIf seeking parenting orders
Proof of value and proof of procedural fairness to the trusteeIf seeking superannuation orders
Copy of existing ordersIf varying or discharging existing orders
De facto registration certificateIf applicable

Parenting and financial orders can be sought in the same application. The Court's FCFCOA fee schedule list the filing fee for an Application for Consent Orders as $215 (from 1 July 2026; GST does not apply to court fees).

Time limits for financial orders

An application can be filed any time after separation, but financial orders should be sought within 12 months of a divorce becoming final, or two years after a de facto relationship ends. Applying later raises additional requirements, so get advice promptly if you are close to either date.

Why proposed orders get sent back

The Court can request more information or decline to make orders. Common reasons, based on the Court's requirements, include:

  • the information does not let the Court assess whether the property orders are just and equitable;
  • parenting orders do not appear to be in the child's best interests, or risk issues are not addressed;
  • assets, debts or super are not identified precisely enough to be carried out;
  • missing superannuation trustee notice or proof of value;
  • missing documents, such as the unsigned Word version of the orders.

What consent orders cannot do

Consent orders are not a catch-all. Child support for children covered by the Child Support (Assessment) Act is generally dealt with through the child support system rather than parenting orders, and orders cannot bind people who are not parties. Agreement also does not override the Court's legal tests. If something you want is unusual, ask a lawyer whether orders can achieve it.

From a mediated agreement to orders

Mediation can help you reach the agreement that consent orders record, but a mediator does not make court orders. A practical sequence is:

  1. Exchange financial disclosure and identify the asset pool.
  2. Reach agreement in principle at mediation on property and/or parenting.
  3. Each party gets independent legal advice on the agreement.
  4. A lawyer drafts the proposed orders on the Court's template.
  5. Serve super trustee notice if needed, then file.

The property settlement mediation guide explains the first two steps in more detail.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Can I copy an example consent order?+

An example can help you understand the layout, but it cannot tell you what orders are appropriate for your family. Use the Court's proposed orders template and ask a lawyer to review the substance before filing.

Are property and parenting consent orders the same?+

No. Parenting orders must be in the child's best interests, while financial or property orders must be just and equitable. Both can be sought in one application, but each needs appropriate supporting information.

What documents are filed with proposed consent orders?+

The Application for Consent Orders, the signed proposed orders and an identical unsigned Word version. Parenting applications also need a Notice of child abuse, family violence or risk; super splits need proof of value and trustee notice.

How much does it cost to file consent orders?+

The Court's filing fee for an Application for Consent Orders is $215 from 1 July 2026. Legal fees for drafting and advice are separate.

Do I need to notify my super fund?+

Yes, if you are seeking a superannuation splitting order. Written notice must be served on the trustee at least 28 days before filing, and the trustee can object within 28 days.

Can mediation produce consent orders?+

Mediation can help people reach an agreement, but the mediator does not make court orders. Parties can get independent legal advice and ask the Court to make orders in agreed terms. The Court still decides whether to make them.

Are there time limits for financial consent orders?+

Financial applications should generally be filed within 12 months after a divorce becomes final, or two years after a de facto relationship ends. Applying later raises additional requirements, so get legal advice promptly.
