When Should You Consider Property Settlement Mediation?

Dan Toombs Founder & Accredited Mediator · AMDRAS · Multi-Award Winning Lawyer

Key takeaway: There is no legal requirement to wait for divorce before starting a property settlement. Strict time limits apply — 12 months after divorce for married couples, 2 years after separation for de facto couples — and early resolution through mediation protects both parties from market movements, asset dissipation, and spiralling legal costs. The right time to start is almost always: now.

You don't need to wait for divorce

One of the most common misunderstandings about family law in Australia is that you must wait for a divorce before dealing with property. This is not correct. Property settlement and divorce are entirely separate legal processes under the Family Law Act 1975.

A divorce is simply the legal dissolution of a marriage — it says nothing about how property is divided, how children are cared for, or what financial obligations exist between the parties. You can finalise your property settlement long before a divorce is granted, or well after. What matters is that it is done within the applicable time limits.

For many separated couples, delaying the property settlement until after divorce means 12 months of unnecessary uncertainty — during which neither party can refinance, sell assets, or make major financial decisions without the other's consent. Starting the property settlement process early avoids this limbo period entirely.

Mediation can begin at any time after separation. In fact, addressing property issues early — while memories are fresh and goodwill (however limited) still exists — often produces better outcomes than waiting until conflict has escalated and legal costs have accumulated.

Strict time limits you cannot ignore

While you can start a property settlement at any time after separation, there are strict limitation periods beyond which you may lose your right to apply to a court:

Relationship typeTime limitStarting from
Married couples12 monthsDate the divorce order becomes final (one month and one day after it is made)
De facto couples2 yearsDate of separation

After these deadlines pass, you need leave of the court — special permission to bring a property settlement claim. Leave is not automatic. The court must be satisfied that hardship would result if leave is refused. Courts are reluctant to grant leave, and the legal costs of applying for it can be significant.

If you are approaching these deadlines, act urgently. Even if you cannot reach agreement in time, filing a court application before the deadline preserves your right to proceed — you can continue mediating after filing. Importantly, for married couples, the 12-month period runs from when the divorce becomes final — not from separation. Many couples have considerably more time than they realise.

When is the right time to start?

The optimal time depends on your circumstances, but as a general guide:

  • Immediately after separation — if there are urgent issues such as who stays in the family home, who services the mortgage, or a genuine risk that one party will dissipate assets
  • Within the first 3–6 months — for most couples, this is the sweet spot. Emotions are still raw but both parties have had time to stabilise. Financial documents from the date of separation are still accessible. Both parties remember the assets and liabilities clearly.
  • Certainly before 12 months for de facto couples — to ensure there is adequate time to negotiate, mediate, and formalise an agreement before the 2-year limit becomes urgent
  • As soon as possible if the other party is dissipating assets — a court can make urgent orders to freeze assets if there is a genuine risk; seek legal advice immediately in this situation

In our experience at Mediations Australia, the couples who resolve their property settlement most smoothly are those who start the process early, before adversarial positions have hardened and legal costs have accumulated.

Risks of delaying a property settlement

Delay carries real financial and legal risks. These include:

  • Asset value changes: Property, business, and investment values fluctuate. A delay may benefit one party at the expense of the other, or significantly complicate the valuation process
  • Asset dissipation: Without a binding agreement, nothing legally prevents one party from spending, gifting, or otherwise reducing the value of jointly-held assets. Courts can look back at these transactions, but recovering value is difficult and expensive
  • Superannuation growth: Post-separation superannuation contributions generally remain with the contributor. The longer the delay, the greater the potential disparity in super balances
  • Joint debt risk: Without an agreement, joint debts (including mortgages) remain the legal responsibility of both parties. If one party stops servicing a joint debt, the other party's credit rating may be affected
  • New complications: New relationships, new assets, and new obligations complicate property settlements over time. The longer you wait, the harder it becomes to draw a clear line around the property pool
  • Time limit risk: Missing the applicable limitation period can eliminate your right to apply to court — severely weakening your negotiating position in mediation

What to do before starting

  • Get legal advice. Understanding your entitlements under the Family Law Act before negotiations begin means you can assess whether what is on the table is fair
  • Gather your financial documents. Compile statements for all assets and liabilities as at the date of separation — this is the starting point for the asset pool assessment
  • Agree on a date of separation. This is the date from which the asset pool is assessed. If you and your ex disagree, this needs to be resolved — sometimes through the mediation process itself
  • Protect urgent issues. If there is an immediate risk of asset dissipation, seek legal advice about urgent court orders before commencing mediation

How mediation speeds up the process

Mediation is the fastest and least expensive pathway to a binding property settlement in Australia. A mediated settlement typically reaches agreement in one to three sessions over a period of days to weeks — compared to 12–24 months for a contested court matter.

At Mediations Australia, both parties complete a financial disclosure statement before the session. The mediator reviews the disclosed information and identifies the key issues. In the joint session, the mediator works through the asset pool, each party's contributions over the relationship, and future needs — exploring options for division until agreement is reached. The written summary produced at the end of the session is then used by family lawyers to draft formal Consent Orders or a Binding Financial Agreement.

Our mediators either are lawyers or have deep legal knowledge of the family law framework. This allows them to help parties understand what a court would likely do — which is enormously helpful in anchoring expectations and reaching a realistic, durable agreement.

What makes a property settlement legally binding?

A verbal agreement or even a written mediation summary is not yet legally binding. To make a property settlement enforceable, it must be formalised through one of two methods:

  • Consent Orders: Filed in the Federal Circuit and Family Court, reviewed by a Registrar, and approved as court orders. No hearing is required in most cases. This is the most common and cost-effective way to formalise a mediated property settlement.
  • Binding Financial Agreement (BFA): A formal contract executed under Part VIIIA of the Family Law Act. Each party must receive independent legal advice before signing. BFAs are more flexible but also more complex and expensive to prepare than Consent Orders.

Your mediator at Mediations Australia will advise on which form of formalisation best suits your situation and will refer you to appropriate legal professionals to complete the process.

Frequently asked questions

Can we start a property settlement while still living together?

Yes. It is possible to formalise a property settlement while still residing in the same property, provided you are genuinely separated. Courts will examine the question of whether separation has actually occurred — particularly for couples still co-habitating — but the mere fact of shared housing does not prevent a property settlement from proceeding.

Do we both have to agree to mediation?

Yes — mediation is voluntary and requires both parties to participate willingly. If the other party refuses to engage, your options include applying directly to court for property matters (no Section 60I certificate is required for property, unlike parenting matters) or seeking legal advice about other ways to encourage engagement.

What if we already have an informal arrangement?

An informal arrangement is better than nothing but is not legally binding. Either party can change their mind. Formalising your arrangement through Consent Orders or a BFA protects both parties and provides legal certainty going forward.

How long does the Consent Orders process take after mediation?

A family lawyer prepares the application, which is signed by both parties and filed with the court. In most cases, Consent Orders are approved by a Registrar within 4–8 weeks of filing, without the need for a court hearing. Your mediator can refer you to an appropriate family lawyer to prepare the documents efficiently.
