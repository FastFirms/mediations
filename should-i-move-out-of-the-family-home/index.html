Should I move out of the family home

When a relationship ends, one of the first practical questions is who stays in the house. Many people worry that leaving means giving up their share of the property or their time with the children. The law is more reassuring than the rumours, but the way you move out still matters. This guide explains what moving out does and does not change, what to agree before you go, and how mediation can settle the interim arrangements without anyone filing in court.

Key takeaway: No, moving out of the family home does not mean you lose your share of it. Ownership and your property settlement entitlements are unchanged. What moving out can affect is the day-to-day care of children, who pays which bills, and your access to records, so agree interim arrangements in writing before you leave. If you are unsafe, leave first and seek help.

Does moving out affect your share of the property?

Moving out does not change who owns the home. If your name is on the title, it stays there until a property settlement or sale changes it. Under the Family Law Act 1975, a property settlement looks at the whole asset pool, each person's contributions over the relationship (financial and non-financial, including caring for children and the home), their future needs and whether the outcome is just and equitable. Who happened to be living in the house on the day of separation is not one of those factors.

What can matter is what happens after you leave. If you keep paying half the mortgage while living elsewhere and also paying rent, that is a post-separation contribution and may be taken into account. If your ex alone pays the mortgage for two years, that may count in their favour. The point is that the money trail after separation is relevant, not the address you sleep at.

General information only. Your circumstances may differ; family lawyers and mediators can advise on your situation.

Moving out and the children

This is where moving out needs the most thought. Parenting decisions are made on what is in the child's best interests, and the law does not favour mothers or fathers. But if you move out and the children stay with the other parent for months, a routine forms. When parents later cannot agree, the arrangement that has been working, sometimes called the status quo, can carry real weight in interim decisions, because courts are reluctant to disrupt a settled child.

That does not mean you should stay in a tense house at any cost. It means you should agree, before or as you move, when the children will spend time with you, and put it in writing. A simple interim parenting plan, signed and dated by you both, records that the arrangement is temporary and was agreed rather than abandoned. Our parenting plans guide explains what to include.

  • Agree a written schedule for the children's time with each parent from the first week.
  • Choose a new home close enough to school and activities to make that schedule realistic.
  • Keep a bedroom or space for the children, even if modest.
  • Keep records of the time you spend with them and your involvement in school and health.
  • Avoid taking the children with you without the other parent's agreement unless there is a safety risk.

Agreeing an interim parenting schedule before you move out protects your relationship with your children. We can help you put one together quickly.

Who pays the mortgage and bills after you leave

Running two households costs more than one, and money pressure is a common source of conflict in the first months. There is no automatic rule about who pays the mortgage after one person moves out. Lenders still expect the full repayment from everyone on the loan, so a missed payment affects both credit files regardless of who lives there.

Common interim arrangements include the person staying paying the mortgage and household bills, the person leaving continuing to contribute to the mortgage while the other pays outgoings, or both continuing as before until a sale. Whatever you choose, record it, and note that the arrangement is without prejudice to the final settlement. Our guide to who pays bills during separation goes through the options in detail.

If there are children, child support is assessed separately by Services Australia — Child Support, using both incomes and the level of care each parent provides. Changes in care after one parent moves can change the assessment, so update Services Australia promptly. If one partner cannot meet their reasonable expenses, interim spousal maintenance may also be relevant.

Money arrangements in the first few months set the tone for everything else. A mediator can help you agree a fair interim budget in a single session.

What to agree and organise before you move out

A planned departure is calmer for everyone, and it leaves you in a much better position later. Before you leave, if it is safe to do so:

  1. Copy financial records. Bank and super statements, tax returns, loan documents, insurance policies and the title or rates notice. Both of you have a duty of full and frank disclosure in a property settlement, and it is far easier to gather documents while you still have access.
  2. Photograph the contents. A quick record of furniture, vehicles and valuables helps avoid disputes about what was there.
  3. Take personal items and essentials. Identity documents, medication, sentimental items and work equipment. Leave shared household items for the settlement.
  4. Agree the children's schedule in writing. See above.
  5. Agree interim money arrangements. Mortgage, bills, joint accounts and credit cards.
  6. Change your passwords and mail. Secure email, banking and phone accounts, and redirect your post.
  7. Note the date of separation. It matters later for divorce (you need 12 months apart) and for working out the property pool.

If neither of you can afford to move

Housing costs mean many couples stay in the same home after deciding to separate, at least for a while. That is legally recognised: you can be separated under one roof as long as the relationship has genuinely ended. If you later apply for divorce, you will need to show the separation was real, usually with evidence of separate finances, separate bedrooms and no longer presenting as a couple.

Living together while separated works best with clear ground rules: who uses which rooms, how the children's routines run, how bills are split and how you will communicate. It can be a sensible bridge while you agree a property settlement and plan a sale or buy-out, but it is not suitable where there is fear, control or violence.

Sharing a home while separated is easier with agreed house rules. Mediation is a practical way to set them without another argument.

When safety means leaving now

Everything above assumes it is safe to plan. If you or your children are at risk, your safety comes first. Leave when you need to, take the children with you if they are in danger, and get help. Leaving because of family violence does not reduce your property entitlements, and courts take family violence seriously in parenting and, since the 2025 reforms, in property matters too.

  • In an emergency, call 000.
  • For confidential support, call 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732 or visit 1800RESPECT.
  • Police and local courts can make protection orders that may exclude a violent person from the home.

Mediation may not be appropriate where there is family violence, and exemptions from family dispute resolution apply. Our guide to domestic violence and family law explains your options.

Can your ex force you out, or stop you returning?

Generally, a partner cannot lawfully lock out a co-owner or a joint tenant without a court order. Changing the locks on a jointly owned home can escalate things quickly and may be challenged. Courts can make orders for one person to have sole use of the home, and protection orders can exclude a person for safety reasons, but these are decided on evidence, not unilaterally. The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia handles applications for such orders where parties cannot agree.

If the home is in one person's sole name or rented in one name, the position can differ, and if you are in Western Australia, de facto matters go through the Family Court of WA. Because the details matter, it is worth getting legal advice if you are being pressured to leave or prevented from returning. Family lawyers and mediators often work together on exactly this kind of interim question.

Settling interim arrangements through mediation

Court has its place, but it is rarely the best place to decide who sleeps where next week. Mediation lets you agree interim arrangements quickly: who stays, the children's schedule, who pays which bills, how you will communicate, and a timeline for the property settlement. These agreements can be recorded in a parenting plan and a written interim agreement, then made binding later through consent orders once the full picture is clear.

Research from the Australian Institute of Family Studies shows that most separated parents sort out their arrangements without a judicial decision, often with the help of family dispute resolution. Starting with mediation keeps costs down and keeps decisions in your hands rather than a stranger's. Read more about family law mediation or our complete separation guide.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Will I lose my share of the house if I move out?+

No. Moving out does not change legal ownership or your entitlement to a property settlement. The settlement looks at contributions, future needs and fairness across the whole relationship, not who stayed in the house.

Does moving out affect custody of the children?+

It can, if the children stay with the other parent and a routine forms without any agreement about your time. Agree a written interim parenting schedule before or as you move to protect your relationship with them.

Do I still have to pay the mortgage if I move out?+

The lender expects the full repayment from everyone on the loan. Between you, who pays is a matter for agreement, and payments made after separation can be taken into account in the final settlement.

Can my ex change the locks if we both own the house?+

Generally a co-owner should not be locked out without a court order. Courts can grant sole occupation and protection orders can exclude a person for safety, but these are decided on evidence. Get legal advice if this happens.

Can we be separated while still living together?+

Yes. You can be separated under one roof if the relationship has genuinely ended. If you later divorce, you will need evidence of the separation, such as separate finances and no longer presenting as a couple.

Can mediation help us decide who stays in the house?+

Yes. Mediation is a fast, low-cost way to agree interim living, parenting and money arrangements, which can later be formalised through consent orders. It may not be suitable where there is family violence.
