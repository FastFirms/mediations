Court-ordered drug and alcohol testing in parenting cases

Drug and alcohol testing can form part of the evidence used to assess risk in parenting proceedings. This guide explains the tests the Court mentions, how testing orders are typically worded, who usually pays, what happens with results, and where mediation and voluntary testing fit.

Key takeaway: In parenting proceedings, drug and alcohol testing is one kind of evidence the Court may use to assess risk to a child; the Court names urine, blood and hair-follicle tests. Testing orders usually set the test type, timing, photo ID, who receives results and who pays. A mediator cannot order a test, though parents can agree to voluntary testing, and no single result automatically decides parenting arrangements.

Why testing arises in parenting cases

Parenting cases turn on the best interests of the child, and child safety sits at the centre of that question. Where one parent raises concerns about the other's drug or alcohol use, the Court may need evidence to assess whether there is a risk to the child, and how serious it is. The Court's guidance on FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters lists the kinds of investigations that may assist it, including drug and alcohol tests.

Allegations, evidence and safety are different things

An allegation is a claim. Evidence is material the Court can weigh. Safety is the outcome everyone should be focused on. Testing can help close the gap between a claim and evidence, but it is one part of the picture, alongside reports, records and other material relevant to the family.

How testing usually comes up

  • By agreement: a parent offers or agrees to test to reassure the other parent, sometimes as part of a parenting plan.
  • By interim order: the Court orders testing while a case is ongoing, often with conditions on time with the children.
  • In final orders: testing continues for a period as a safeguard.

Can a mediator order a drug test?

No. A mediator, including a family dispute resolution practitioner, helps people explore possible agreements. A mediator does not make binding orders and cannot require anyone to be tested. What mediation can do is give parents a structured, safe space to discuss whether voluntary testing would help, and what it would look like: which test, how often, who pays, who sees results and what happens next.

Voluntary testing agreed in mediation can be recorded in a parenting plan or, with legal advice, in consent orders. A parenting plan is not enforceable like a court order, so if testing is critical to safety, ask a lawyer whether orders are more appropriate. If there is already a court order requiring testing, its terms govern; ask a lawyer what it requires.

Types of testing the Court mentions

The Court's FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters page lists drug and/or alcohol tests, including urine tests, blood tests and/or hair follicle tests. Example orders published by the Family Court of WA: Examples of parenting orders show how these are used in practice.

TestHow it appears in court examplesWhat to ask a clinician or provider
Urine (urinalysis)Supervised "broadscreen" tests, either once within a set time or randomly on requestWhat substances are screened, how collection is supervised and verified
Hair strandTesting for named substance groups, with an order not to cut or colour hair beforehandWhat period a result relates to and how it should be interpreted
Blood (including alcohol markers)Used where alcohol use is the concernWhat the marker measures, and its limitations

Detection periods and accuracy vary by test, substance, laboratory and individual. We deliberately do not quote detection windows here: ask the testing provider or a clinician, and treat figures on commercial websites with caution.

What is a CDT test?

CDT stands for carbohydrate-deficient transferrin, a blood marker used as an indicator of sustained heavy alcohol use. A peer-reviewed review describes it as widely recognised as a reliable biomarker of chronic alcohol abuse, while noting that questions about its interpretation remain (Bortolotti et al., CDT as a marker of alcohol abuse (J Chromatogr B, 2006)). In practice that means a CDT result is evidence to be interpreted by a qualified professional, not a stand-alone answer. Ask a clinician what a particular result can and cannot show.

How testing orders are typically worded

The Family Court of Western Australia publishes example parenting orders, including testing orders. Its examples show the elements courts commonly specify (paraphrased):

  • A one-off urinalysis: a supervised broadscreen urine test with photographic identification within 24 hours or by a set time, with results released to the other party, their lawyer, the Independent Children's Lawyer or the Family Consultant.
  • Hair strand testing: testing for named substance groups as soon as practicable, at the cost of the party tested, with an injunction against cutting or colouring hair before the test.
  • Random urinalysis: tests with photo ID within 24 hours of a written or SMS request, with the cost borne by the party tested, and an obligation to keep the other party informed of contact numbers.
  • Consequences of clear results: time arrangements that change after consecutive clear results with a complete chain of custody.

Source: Family Court of WA: Examples of parenting orders. These are examples from WA; the federal Court's orders vary with each case, so ask a lawyer about the right terms for yours.

What any testing arrangement should cover

Whether testing is ordered or agreed, vague arrangements cause disputes. A clear arrangement covers:

ElementQuestion to settle
ProviderWhich independent testing provider or pathology service?
Test typeUrine, hair, blood, or a combination, and for which substances?
IdentityPhoto ID and supervised collection?
Chain of custodyHow is the sample tracked from collection to result?
TimingOne-off, scheduled or random? How quickly after a request?
DurationFor how long, and what ends the testing?
ResultsWho receives them and in what form?
CostWho pays, and is it shared?
ConsequencesWhat changes after clear, positive or missed tests?
PrivacyHow is sensitive health information stored and shared?

Who pays for testing?

The federal Court says that in most cases the costs of expert services and investigations, other than those provided by the Court's own Children's Services, are paid by the parties (FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters). The WA example orders commonly place the cost on the party being tested. Actual fees depend on the provider, the test and how often it is done, so get a written quote. If you are legally aided, ask Legal Aid whether testing costs can be funded in your matter. Mediation fees do not include testing or expert reports.

Results, refusal and disputed evidence

A test result is not an automatic parenting outcome. The Court considers child safety and risk using all the evidence appropriate to the case. Parents in this situation commonly face questions such as:

  • A positive result: what does it show, and over what period? A clinician can interpret it; a lawyer can explain how it may be used.
  • A missed or refused test: how might the Court view it under the order? Ask a lawyer before deciding not to test.
  • A disputed result: was the chain of custody complete? Is a confirmatory test available?

Do not draw conclusions from a single result or a general guide, including this one.

Child protection agency testing vs family court proceedings

This guide addresses testing in family law parenting proceedings. A state or territory child protection agency operates under a different legal framework, and its requirements differ between jurisdictions. There is no single nationwide child protection drug testing policy. If an agency is involved, ask that agency and a qualified lawyer about its requirements.

When mediation is unsafe or inappropriate

Where there are serious safety concerns, including family violence or risk to a child, mediation may not be appropriate and exemptions from family dispute resolution can apply. See is family law mediation compulsory? and our guide to domestic violence and family law. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 000. For confidential support, contact 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Can a mediator order a drug test?+

No. A mediator helps participants explore possible agreements and does not make binding orders. Parents can agree to voluntary testing in mediation; if testing is critical to safety, get legal advice about whether court orders are more appropriate.

What kinds of tests can be relevant?+

The Court lists urine, blood and hair-follicle tests among investigations that may assist its assessment of risk. A clinician can explain a test's purpose and limitations; a lawyer can explain its role in proceedings.

What is a CDT test?+

CDT (carbohydrate-deficient transferrin) is a blood marker used as an indicator of sustained heavy alcohol use. Its interpretation needs a qualified professional; it is not a stand-alone answer.

How are drug testing orders usually worded?+

Published example orders specify the test type, supervised collection with photo ID, timing (for example within 24 hours of a request), who receives results, who pays and, for hair tests, no cutting or colouring hair beforehand.

Who pays for drug or alcohol testing?+

The Court says that, in most cases, the parties pay for expert services and investigations other than those provided by Court Children's Services. Example orders often place the cost on the party tested.

Does a positive result automatically decide parenting arrangements?+

No. The Court considers child safety and risk using the evidence appropriate to the case. Get legal advice about the evidence and clinical advice about interpretation.

Is child-protection drug testing the same as family court testing?+

No. A state or territory child protection agency operates under a different legal framework from family law proceedings. Ask that agency and a qualified lawyer about its requirements.
