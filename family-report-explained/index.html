Family reports explained: the assessment that shapes parenting orders

A family report is one of the most influential documents in a parenting dispute. Here is what happens during the assessment, how to prepare, and why settling in mediation can mean you never need one.

Key takeaway: A family report is a written assessment by a family consultant (usually a psychologist or social worker) ordered by the court in parenting disputes. The consultant interviews both parents and the children, observes interactions, and makes recommendations about what parenting arrangements are in the child's best interests. Family reports carry significant weight with judges. Understanding the process, and preparing well, can make a real difference to the outcome.

What is a family report?

A family report is an independent, expert assessment prepared under section 11F of the Family Law Act 1975. The court orders a family report when it needs an objective view of the family's circumstances before making parenting orders. The report is prepared by a family consultant — a qualified psychologist, social worker or counsellor appointed by the court.

The report typically covers:

  • each parent's relationship with the child;
  • the child's wishes, expressed in age-appropriate ways;
  • each parent's capacity to provide for the child's needs;
  • any risks, including family violence, substance misuse or mental health concerns;
  • the child's developmental, emotional and educational needs; and
  • recommendations about living arrangements, time and decision-making.

Family reports are used in both interim (temporary) and final hearings. They are one of the most influential pieces of evidence in parenting proceedings — judges rely heavily on the consultant's professional assessment.

When does the court order a family report?

The court can order a family report at any stage of parenting proceedings, but it most commonly happens:

  • at the first court event (such as a case assessment conference or procedural hearing), when the judge identifies that the dispute needs expert input;
  • where there are allegations of family violence, abuse or risk to the child;
  • where parents have very different accounts of the child's wishes or needs;
  • where a child has special needs, a disability or complex health issues; and
  • where there is a significant dispute about relocation or sole parental responsibility.

The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia manages the family report process through its Child Dispute Services. Court-ordered family reports are prepared at no cost to the parties — they are funded by the court. If you want a report outside the court process, you can commission a private family report from an independent psychologist, but these cost $5,000–$15,000 or more.

What happens during a family report assessment

The family report process typically follows these steps:

  1. Court order. The judge orders the report and specifies the issues it should address.
  2. Appointment scheduling. Child Dispute Services contacts both parents to arrange interviews, usually within 4–8 weeks of the order.
  3. Individual interviews. The consultant meets each parent separately for 1–2 hours. They ask about your relationship with the child, your concerns, your proposed arrangements, and your view of the other parent's strengths and weaknesses.
  4. Child interview. The consultant speaks to the child (if old enough) in a child-friendly setting, using play, drawing or conversation to understand the child's experience.
  5. Observation sessions. The consultant may observe each parent interacting with the child, often at the court's premises.
  6. Collateral contacts. The consultant may speak to teachers, GPs, therapists or other relevant people, with the parents' consent.
  7. Report writing. The consultant writes the report and files it with the court. Both parties receive a copy, usually 4–8 weeks after the last interview.

Indicative timeframes only. Wait times vary by court location and consultant availability.

How to prepare for a family report interview

Your interview with the family consultant is not a test, but it is an opportunity to show that you are a thoughtful, child-focused parent. Practical preparation includes:

  • Focus on the child. Talk about your child's needs, routine, friendships, school and interests — not just the conflict with the other parent.
  • Be honest. Consultants are trained to spot exaggeration. Acknowledge your own imperfections and the other parent's strengths where they exist.
  • Stay calm and respectful. How you present matters. Anger, hostility towards the other parent, or badmouthing them will be noted.
  • Bring evidence quietly. If there are safety concerns (family violence, substance misuse), bring police reports, intervention orders or medical records. Do not dump a folder of trivial complaints.
  • Prepare your proposed arrangements. Be ready to explain what schedule you want, why it suits the child, and how you would handle transitions, holidays and decision-making.
  • Do not coach your child. Consultants can tell when a child has been coached, and it reflects badly on the parent who did it. Let your child speak freely.

If your child is seeing a psychologist or counsellor, let the consultant know — with consent, they may contact that professional for context.

What the family consultant is looking for

The consultant assesses the family against the best-interests factors in section 60CC of the Family Law Act 1975. Since the 2024 amendments, the primary consideration is:

  • Safety. The safety of the child and of each parent or carer. Family violence, abuse and risk come first.

Additional considerations include:

  • the views of the child, given weight according to their age and maturity;
  • the developmental, psychological, emotional and cultural needs of the child;
  • the capacity of each parent to provide for those needs;
  • the benefit to the child of a relationship with both parents, where it is safe;
  • each parent's attitude to the child's relationship with the other parent; and
  • anything else that is relevant.

Consultants also look for patterns rather than isolated incidents: a parent who has consistently attended school events, medical appointments and extracurricular activities demonstrates involvement more credibly than one who starts doing so only after proceedings begin.

Can you challenge a family report?

A family report is evidence, not a court order. It carries significant weight, but it is not binding. If you disagree with the report's findings or recommendations, you can:

  • Cross-examine the consultant at the hearing. Your lawyer (or you, if self-represented) can ask the consultant to explain their methodology, challenge their conclusions, and test whether they considered all the evidence.
  • Commission a private report from an independent psychologist. The court may give it less weight than its own report, but it provides an alternative expert view.
  • Present other evidence that contradicts the report, such as school records, medical evidence, or testimony from people who know the family well.
  • Ask the court for a supplementary report if there has been a significant change in circumstances since the original report was prepared.

Challenging a family report is expensive and difficult. Courts start from a position of trusting their own consultants. It is worth challenging only if there is a genuine error or if the consultant missed something important — not simply because the report favours the other parent.

Private family reports

Parents sometimes commission a private family report (also called a single expert report) outside the court process. This can be useful if:

  • you are in mediation and want an independent expert view to inform the negotiation;
  • you want to understand what a court-ordered report might say before you decide whether to litigate;
  • there is a specific clinical question, such as the impact of a parent's mental health on the child.

Indicative only. Private report costs depend on the expert and the complexity of the case.

A private report is strongest when both parents agree to participate. A report based on only one parent's interview and the child's interview carries less weight because the consultant has not assessed the other parent.

Settling in mediation to avoid a family report altogether

A family report is only necessary when parents cannot agree. If you can resolve your parenting dispute in mediation, you avoid the report entirely — and the months of waiting, the stress of being assessed, and the risk of an unfavourable recommendation.

Mediation lets you:

  • design parenting arrangements that reflect your child's actual needs, not a consultant's snapshot;
  • address the concerns that would otherwise be raised in a report — safety, communication, routine — directly;
  • use shuttle mediation if you cannot be in the same room as the other parent;
  • agree a parenting plan or draft consent orders that become binding once filed; and
  • include review points so the arrangement evolves as your child grows.

The Attorney-General's Dept — Family Dispute Resolution explains the role of family dispute resolution before parenting applications. Even if proceedings have started, it is never too late to mediate — many parents settle after a family report is ordered but before it is completed. Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

What is a family report in Australian family law?+

A family report is an independent assessment by a court-appointed family consultant — usually a psychologist or social worker — who interviews both parents and the children and makes recommendations about parenting arrangements in the child's best interests.

How long does a family report take?+

From the court order to the finished report, expect 10 to 22 weeks. Wait times depend on court location and consultant availability.

Does a family report decide the outcome?+

No, the report is evidence, not a decision. However, judges give it significant weight because it represents an independent expert assessment. It is one of the most influential documents in parenting proceedings.

Can I challenge a family report I disagree with?+

Yes. You can cross-examine the consultant at the hearing, commission a private report, or present other evidence. However, challenging a court report is expensive and courts trust their own process, so it is worth doing only if there is a genuine error or omission.

How much does a private family report cost?+

A private report from an independent psychologist typically costs $5,000 to $15,000 or more, depending on the expert and the complexity of the case. Court-ordered reports are free.

Can I avoid a family report by settling in mediation?+

Yes. A family report is only needed when parents cannot agree. If you resolve your dispute in mediation, you skip the report entirely and can formalise your agreement as a parenting plan or consent orders.
