Pre-Separation Checklist: What to Do Before You Leave

Dan Toombs Founder & Accredited Mediator · AMDRAS · Multi-Award Winning Lawyer

Key takeaway: Taking practical legal and financial steps before you separate can significantly protect your position — without being dishonest or unfair. Gathering your own financial records, understanding your entitlements, and making a safe plan puts you in a far better position than leaving without preparation. This checklist covers what to do before the conversation happens.

A critical note first

Preparing for separation is legitimate and important. But the steps below have limits. Preparation means understanding your position and protecting legitimate access to shared information — it does not mean hiding assets, emptying joint accounts, concealing property, or taking steps that would breach the financial disclosure duties that apply once you separate.

Once you separate, both parties have a legal duty to make full financial disclosure in any property settlement process. Any assets you conceal can be the subject of court orders to recover them, and deliberate concealment can result in serious adverse consequences. Preparation is about protecting your position — not improving it by dishonest means.

If you are in a situation involving family violence, your safety is the first priority. See the safety section below before anything else.

Financial preparation

  • Know what you have. Before you separate, get a clear picture of the shared financial position — assets, liabilities, superannuation, investments. If you do not have access to financial records, start gathering what you can now. Once separation is announced, access to joint records may become difficult.
  • Open a personal bank account in your name only if you do not already have one — at a bank separate from your joint banking. You will need your own financial base from the point of separation.
  • Understand your ongoing income. What income do you earn directly? If you rely on a partner's income, what are your immediate financial needs post-separation?
  • Do NOT empty joint accounts. Taking more than your immediate needs from joint accounts before separation is risky legally and can be treated as dissipation of assets in property proceedings.
  • Do NOT rack up debt on joint credit cards. Debts incurred for personal benefit after the relationship has effectively ended may be treated differently in a property settlement.

Documents to gather and copy

Before you separate (or immediately after, while access is available), make copies of:

  • Your own identity documents — passport, driver's licence, birth certificate
  • Children's birth certificates and passports
  • Marriage certificate or evidence of de facto relationship
  • Recent bank statements for all joint and individual accounts
  • Mortgage statements and property records
  • Superannuation member statements for both parties
  • Tax returns for the past 2–3 years for both parties
  • Business financial statements if any party has a business interest
  • Vehicle registration documents
  • Share portfolio and investment statements
  • Any pre-nuptial or financial agreements
  • Wills — yours and your partner's

You are entitled to access records about shared assets and finances. The purpose is to ensure you have an accurate record of the financial position at the time of separation.

Protect your personal accounts and digital access

Refer to our guide on protecting your online privacy after separation for detailed steps. In brief, before or immediately after separating:

  • Change passwords on your personal email accounts
  • Review cloud storage — remove shared access and secure your own data
  • Disable location sharing on your phone and connected apps
  • Review your superannuation beneficiary nominations — these can be updated at any time
  • Review your will and any powers of attorney — you may want to update these after separation

Housing and living arrangements

One of the most immediate practical questions in separation is who stays in the family home. Important points:

  • Both parties generally have a legal right to remain in the family home after separation, regardless of whose name is on the title, unless a court order says otherwise
  • If you intend to leave, be aware that voluntarily vacating the home does not mean you lose your property rights in it — but it may affect parenting arrangements if children remain
  • If you need the other party to leave but they refuse, a court order may be required
  • Consider where you would live if you were the party to leave — having a plan before the separation conversation is practical

Housing and financial arrangements post-separation are best resolved through mediation — quickly, privately, and without the cost of court. Talk to us.

If you have children

  • Do not remove the children from the family home before reaching agreement on temporary parenting arrangements — unilateral removal creates conflict and may result in urgent court proceedings
  • Do not take children interstate or overseas without the other parent's written consent — this can constitute parental abduction
  • Plan who will have the children on which days from day one — having a clear, agreed temporary arrangement from the start reduces conflict and protects the children's stability
  • Consider early parenting mediation — arranging FDR early allows temporary parenting arrangements to be agreed before conflict escalates

Legal steps to take early

  • Get independent legal advice — even a 1–2 hour consultation with a family lawyer clarifies your rights and the realistic range of outcomes before you start any negotiation
  • Understand the time limits — for married couples, 12 months from divorce; for de facto couples, 2 years from separation. Do not let these pass without either reaching a formal agreement or filing protective proceedings
  • Update your will — review whether your current will reflects your wishes post-separation
  • Review superannuation beneficiary nominations — super does not automatically form part of your estate and passes to nominated beneficiaries. Update these as soon as possible after separation.
  • Register any informal agreements in writing — if you reach initial agreements about finances or parenting arrangements verbally, confirm them in writing (email is sufficient) as a contemporaneous record

If there is family violence

If you are experiencing family violence, your safety is the absolute first priority. Everything else in this checklist comes after.

  • Emergency: If you are in immediate danger, call 000
  • Support: 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) — 24/7 counselling and safety planning
  • Safety planning: If you are planning to leave a violent situation, speak with 1800RESPECT or a family violence service first — safe departure planning is specialist work
  • Legal protection: Speak to a lawyer about Domestic Violence Orders and whether interim parenting or property orders are needed urgently
  • FDR exemption: Mediation is generally not appropriate where there is family violence. You may not need to attempt FDR before filing for parenting orders — seek legal advice about the exemptions available

A safe separation is the only separation. Do not let financial or legal concerns prevent you from prioritising your physical safety.

Frequently asked questions

When does separation legally begin in Australia?

Separation begins when at least one party communicates to the other that the relationship is over, with no intention of resuming it. For married couples, separation does not require a court order — it is a factual state. You can be legally separated while living under the same roof if it can be shown you are no longer in a relationship.

Do I need to move out to be legally separated?

No. You can be legally separated while remaining in the same home. However, you may need to demonstrate the separation to a court or for divorce proceedings — this typically involves evidence that you have separate finances, sleep in separate rooms, and have stopped presenting to others as a couple.

What is the date of separation?

The date of separation is typically the date on which one party communicated clearly to the other that the relationship was over. The date of separation matters for property time limits and for divorce (12 months separation required before divorce can be applied for).

Should I tell my children before I make these preparations?

No — involve children in the separation process at the appropriate time and in an age-appropriate way, but not before the separation is decided and preparations are in place. When you do tell them, ideally both parents do so together, with a clear, united message about what will happen.
