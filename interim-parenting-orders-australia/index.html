Interim parenting orders in Australia

When parents separate and cannot agree on where the children will live or how much time they spend with each parent, an interim parenting order can set temporary arrangements while the case works its way through the family court system. This guide explains what interim orders are, when they are granted, how to apply, and why mediation is usually a faster and less costly first step.

In most cases, mediation and a parenting plan or consent orders resolve the dispute faster and at a fraction of the cost.

What is an interim parenting order?

An interim parenting order is a temporary order made by the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia that sets out parenting arrangements — where children live, the time they spend with each parent, and sometimes communication arrangements — until the case reaches a final hearing or the parents reach agreement.

The word "interim" simply means "in the meantime." The order is not a preview of the final result. It is designed to keep children safe and to maintain a reasonable status quo while the court considers the full evidence. An interim order can be varied or replaced at any time if circumstances change.

Most interim hearings are short, often one to two hours, and the judge or magistrate works with limited evidence — usually affidavits rather than cross-examination. That means the court is cautious: it leans towards preserving the arrangements the children already know unless there is a good reason not to.

When courts grant interim orders

The court does not grant interim parenting orders simply because one parent asks. There generally needs to be a reason why the matter cannot wait for a final hearing or be resolved by agreement. Common situations include:

  • Safety concerns: allegations of family violence, drug or alcohol misuse, or neglect that put a child at risk.
  • One parent withholding the child: refusing to return a child after a visit, or preventing contact altogether.
  • Relocation: a parent planning to move interstate or overseas with the children before a final hearing.
  • No existing arrangements: parents have just separated and cannot agree on basic logistics.
  • Breach of an existing order: one parent is not following a final or earlier interim order.

Where there is no urgency and the children are safe, the court will usually expect the parents to try FCFCOA family dispute resolution before listing the case for an interim hearing.

If your situation isn't urgent, mediation can often resolve parenting arrangements in a single session — without the stress and cost of court.

How to apply for interim parenting orders

The process depends on whether you already have a case before the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia or are starting fresh.

In genuinely urgent cases — for example, where a child is at immediate risk — the court can hear an application on the same day or the next, sometimes without notice to the other parent. These are rare and require strong evidence of urgency.

What the court considers

The court decides interim parenting orders using the same overarching principle as final orders: the best interests of the child under the Family Law Act 1975. In practice, the court pays particular attention to:

  • The child's safety: Any risk of family violence, abuse or neglect is the court's primary concern. The FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters page explains how this is assessed.
  • The status quo: Where has the child been living? What routine are they used to? Courts are reluctant to disrupt a child's day-to-day life unless there is a strong reason.
  • The child's relationship with each parent: Maintaining meaningful time with both parents matters, unless safety prevents it.
  • Practical arrangements: School proximity, each parent's work schedule, and the child's existing commitments.
  • Any existing agreements or orders: A parenting plan or previous court order carries weight.

The court is not hearing the full case at this stage. It works with limited evidence and makes the best temporary decision it can. An interim order does not prejudice the final outcome, although in practice the status quo it creates can be difficult to shift later.

Recovery orders: when a child is withheld

If a parent is refusing to return a child after agreed time, or is withholding a child from the other parent altogether, the court can make a recovery order under the Family Law Act 1975 (section 67Q). A recovery order directs the Australian Federal Police or state police to find the child and return them to the person named in the order.

Recovery orders are serious. They are usually made urgently and can be applied for at the same time as interim parenting orders. The court needs to be satisfied that the child has been taken or retained, and that making the order is in the child's best interests.

Why mediation is usually the better first step

Unless there is a genuine safety risk or an emergency, parenting mediation is almost always worth trying before applying for interim orders. The reasons are practical:

  • Speed: Mediation can be arranged in days. An interim hearing takes weeks or months.
  • Cost: A mediation session costs a fraction of an interim hearing. See our mediation costs guide.
  • Control: Parents decide the arrangements, rather than a judge who has seen one affidavit from each side.
  • Enforceability: An agreement reached in mediation can be turned into consent orders and is then just as enforceable as a court-imposed order.
  • Relationship: Mediation preserves the co-parenting relationship. Contested court applications tend to damage it.

Where FCFCOA family dispute resolution is required, attempting mediation also fulfils the Section 60I certificate requirement. If mediation does not resolve everything, narrowing the issues still makes any court process shorter and cheaper.

Safety first. If there is family violence, threats, or a risk to your child's safety, mediation may not be appropriate and you may be exempt from FCFCOA family dispute resolution. Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

What is an interim parenting order in Australia?+

An interim parenting order is a temporary court order that sets out where children live and how they spend time with each parent while the case is being decided. It is made by the Federal Circuit and Family Court and stays in place until a final order is made or the parents reach agreement.

How long does it take to get an interim parenting order?+

Typically 4 to 12 weeks from filing, depending on the court registry and how busy the list is. In genuinely urgent cases involving safety risks, the court can hear applications within days or even on the same day.

How much does an interim parenting order cost?+

The court filing fee is around $390, but total legal costs including lawyer preparation and the hearing typically run between $5,000 and $15,000 per party. Mediation costs a fraction of this and can often resolve the dispute without court.

Can I get an interim parenting order without mediation?+

In some cases, yes. Exemptions from family dispute resolution apply where there is family violence, child abuse, urgency, or where one party is unable to participate. Otherwise, a Section 60I certificate showing that mediation was attempted or is not appropriate is generally required before filing.

Do interim parenting orders become permanent?+

Not automatically. An interim order is legally temporary and does not prejudice the final outcome. However, the status quo it creates can carry practical weight at a final hearing, so getting the arrangements right from the start matters.

Can mediation replace an interim parenting order?+

Often, yes. If there is no safety emergency, parents can agree arrangements in mediation and have them made into consent orders, which are just as enforceable as court-imposed orders. This is typically faster, cheaper, and less damaging to the co-parenting relationship.
