Parallel parenting after high conflict

When every handover becomes an argument, parallel parenting gives children two stable homes while keeping parents' contact to a minimum. This guide explains how it works in Australia, what to put in the plan, and how to agree it without court.

Key takeaway: Parallel parenting is a structured way of raising children after separation where each parent runs their own household with minimal direct contact, and communication is limited, written and child-focused. It suits high-conflict separations where co-parenting keeps breaking down, and works best when the rules are agreed in a detailed parenting plan, often through mediation.

What is parallel parenting?

Parallel parenting is an approach for separated parents who cannot yet communicate without conflict. Instead of trying to co-parent closely, attending events together, making decisions over the phone and negotiating every change, each parent takes responsibility for the children during their own time and the two households run largely independently.

The aim is simple: take the children out of the line of fire. Research from the Australian Institute of Family Studies has long shown that children are harmed most by ongoing, unresolved conflict between their parents. Parallel parenting reduces the number of moments where that conflict can happen, while still allowing children to have a meaningful relationship with both parents.

It is not a legal category in the Family Law Act 1975. It is a practical way of structuring a parenting plan or parenting orders, and courts and mediators in Australia regularly use its building blocks.

Parallel parenting vs co-parenting

Co-parenting is the ideal many families aim for, but it relies on parents being able to cooperate flexibly. Parallel parenting is often a stage on the way there, not a permanent label.

When parallel parenting is the right choice

Parallel parenting suits families where both parents are capable and loving, but where contact between the adults keeps escalating. Common signs include:

  • handovers regularly end in arguments, often in front of the children;
  • text messages quickly turn hostile or are used to relitigate the relationship;
  • children are being used to carry messages between homes;
  • every small change to the schedule becomes a dispute;
  • one or both parents feel unable to attend school events while the other is there.

It is especially useful in the first year or two after separation, when hurt and anger are at their highest. Parents who have been described as "high conflict" are often simply in the rawest stage of separation, and structure gives them space to recover. What a parallel parenting plan should include

Because parallel parenting relies on minimal negotiation, the plan has to do the work. The more detail it contains, the fewer decisions you need to make together later. A good plan covers:

  1. The schedule. A clear, repeating pattern of time, plus school holidays, Christmas, birthdays, Mother's Day and Father's Day.
  2. Handovers. Where, when and how, ideally at school or childcare so parents rarely meet, with a neutral backup location.
  3. Communication rules. One agreed channel (for example a co-parenting app or email), expected response times, and a rule that messages are about the children only.
  4. Decision-making. Which major long-term issues, such as schooling, health and religion, require joint consultation, and how disagreements will be resolved.
  5. Day-to-day authority. Confirmation that each parent makes routine decisions during their own time.
  6. Information sharing. Both parents can contact schools and doctors directly, and urgent medical matters are reported promptly.
  7. Events. How school concerts, sport and parent-teacher interviews will be shared or alternated.
  8. Review and dispute resolution. A date to review the plan and an agreement to return to mediation before going to court.

Communication rules that actually work

Most parallel parenting succeeds or fails on communication. A few simple habits make a large difference:

  • Use one written channel. A dedicated co-parenting app keeps a time-stamped record, holds a shared calendar and can track expenses. See our round-up of the best apps for separated parents.
  • Keep it brief, informative, friendly and firm. State the fact, the request and the deadline. Leave out commentary on the past.
  • Agree response times. For example, within 48 hours for routine matters and the same day for urgent ones.
  • Never use the children as messengers. Even small messages put children in a loyalty bind.
  • Pause before replying. If a message makes you angry, wait before you answer. You do not need to respond to every provocation.

Written communication also creates a reliable record if a dispute does arise later, which tends to keep both parents' messages more measured. Free information and referrals are available through Family Relationships Online.

How mediation helps set up parallel parenting

Parents in high conflict often assume they cannot mediate. In practice, mediation is often where parallel parenting begins. An accredited family dispute resolution practitioner can run the process as shuttle mediation, moving between separate rooms or separate online sessions, so you never have to deal with each other directly.

In mediation you can agree the schedule, handover logistics, communication rules and decision-making framework in one structured process, then record it as a parenting plan or, if you want it to be enforceable, as consent orders. Before most parenting applications can be filed, separated parents must also attempt family dispute resolution and obtain a section 60I certificate, unless an exemption applies (FCFCOA family dispute resolution).

Court has its place, particularly where safety is at risk or one parent will not engage at all. But a contested parenting case can take well over a year to reach a final hearing and usually increases conflict. Mediation is rarely the slower or more expensive option, and it lets you design arrangements that suit your family rather than having them imposed.

When parallel parenting is not appropriate

Parallel parenting is designed for conflict between two safe parents. It is not a solution to family violence, coercive control or child abuse. Where there is violence or a risk to children, the focus must shift to safety: supervised arrangements, protection orders and specialist legal advice.

Mediation may not be appropriate where there is family violence, and exemptions from family dispute resolution apply. If you are in danger, call 000. For confidential support, contact 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732. Our guides to domestic violence and family law and supervised visitation explain the options, and the court's approach to risk is set out in FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters.

Moving from parallel parenting to co-parenting

For many families, parallel parenting is a bridge. Once handovers have been calm for several months and written communication has settled into a predictable rhythm, parents often find they can loosen the structure, for example by both attending a school concert, agreeing small schedule swaps by message, or sitting down together at a review mediation.

There is no deadline for this, and some families stay with a parallel model until the children are grown, which is perfectly fine. What matters is that the children experience two stable homes and are no longer caught in the middle. Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between parallel parenting and co-parenting?+

Co-parenting involves frequent, flexible cooperation between parents. Parallel parenting keeps direct contact to a minimum: each parent runs their own household, communication is written and limited to the children, and handovers happen at school or a neutral place.

Is parallel parenting recognised by Australian courts?+

It is not a separate legal category, but its elements, such as handovers at school, communication via an app and clear decision-making rules, are commonly included in parenting plans and consent orders.

Can we mediate if we can't be in the same room?+

Yes. Shuttle mediation lets the mediator move between separate rooms or online sessions, so you can agree a parallel parenting plan without dealing with each other directly.

Is parallel parenting bad for children?+

No. For high-conflict families it is often better for children than attempting close co-parenting, because it reduces their exposure to conflict while preserving their relationship with both parents.

Is parallel parenting suitable if there is family violence?+

No. Parallel parenting is for conflict between safe parents. Where there is violence or risk to children, safety measures and specialist legal advice come first, and exemptions from family dispute resolution apply.
