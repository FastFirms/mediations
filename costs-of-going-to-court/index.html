The Real Cost of Going to Court in Australia

Dan Toombs Founder & Accredited Mediator · AMDRAS · Multi-Award Winning Lawyer

Key takeaway: A contested family law matter in Australia typically costs each party $30,000–$100,000 in legal fees alone, takes one to three years, and hands all decisions to a judge. For most disputes, mediation resolves the same issues in weeks for a fraction of that cost — with an agreement you control.

Court filing fees

The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA) charges filing fees at every stage. These are government fees — separate from anything you pay a lawyer — and they add up quickly.

Application typeFee (2026)
Initiating application (financial/parenting)$365
Response to initiating application$185
Each interim hearing day$545
First trial day$1,090
Each subsequent trial day$545
Subpoena (per subpoena)$55

A case that runs to a three-day trial — not unusual for contested property settlements — will accumulate $2,725 in court fees alone, before a single hour of legal time is charged. Hardship exemptions exist but are narrowly applied.

Lawyer and barrister fees

Court fees are the smallest item on the bill. Solicitor and barrister fees dwarf them.

Solicitor fees for family law matters in Australia typically run $350–$600 per hour depending on experience and city. A solicitor will spend time on correspondence, disclosure, court documents, conferences, and preparation — easily 50–150 hours on a contested matter before the trial begins.

Barrister fees are charged separately. For a hearing day, a family law barrister charges $3,000–$8,000 per day. Preparation — reading briefs, drafting submissions — adds further fees at $400–$800 per hour.

Hidden costs most people miss

The line items above are visible. These costs often go unbudgeted:

  • Subpoenas and third-party disclosure. Obtaining bank records, superannuation statements, business records, and tax documents each generates subpoena fees, compliance costs, and solicitor time to review.
  • Independent children's lawyer (ICL). Where the court appoints an ICL, both parties typically share the cost: $5,000–$15,000 in total.
  • Lost income. Attending hearings, conferences, and meetings means time away from work. A three-day trial costs three days of income — plus preparation days either side.
  • Ongoing housing costs. While proceedings drag on, you may maintain two households instead of one. For a case lasting 18 months, that duplication is real money.
  • Mental health support. Litigation is psychologically gruelling. Counselling, therapy, and GP visits are a real cost of contested court proceedings that never appears in a legal bill.
  • Cost orders against you. If a judge finds your conduct unreasonable — refusing a reasonable offer, for example — they can order you to pay part of the other side's costs. This is rare but real.

What a contested case actually costs

Aggregating court fees, solicitor fees, barrister fees, and expert reports, a contested family law property settlement in Australia typically costs each party:

Case complexityEach party's legal costsTotal (both parties combined)
Simple (2–3 hearing days, consent possible)$15,000–$35,000$30,000–$70,000
Moderate (4–8 hearing days, some contest)$35,000–$70,000$70,000–$140,000
Complex (business assets, business valuation, trial)$70,000–$150,000+$140,000–$300,000+

That money comes from the asset pool. A couple with $600,000 in assets can spend $100,000 in combined legal fees — reducing the pool available to both parties by one sixth before it is divided.

The cost of time

The FCFCOA is under significant pressure. Wait times between filing and a final hearing vary but a contested trial — when parties cannot settle — routinely takes 12–24 months from filing to judgment in most registries. Complex cases take longer.

During that period:

  • Property cannot be sold or refinanced without both parties' consent or a court order
  • Superannuation remains unsplit
  • Business decisions are constrained
  • Emotional closure is deferred
  • Legal bills accumulate at every interlocutory step

Children's matters resolve faster in many cases — the court prioritises interim parenting orders — but final parenting orders after a contested hearing can still take 12+ months.

Emotional and relationship cost

Litigation is adversarial by design. Each side builds a case against the other. Evidence of bad conduct, parenting failures, financial dishonesty, or infidelity may be placed before a judge. Once documents are filed and positions hardened, co-parenting relationships — which must survive for years — are often seriously damaged.

Research consistently shows that parties who negotiate their own agreements (through mediation or direct negotiation) report higher satisfaction, greater compliance, and better co-parenting outcomes than those whose arrangements are imposed by a judge. A court order tells you what you must do; a mediated agreement reflects what you agreed to do. The difference in how it feels — and how it holds — is significant.

"The cases I see in court are rarely ones where court was the only option. Most could have been resolved earlier, with less damage, if both parties had engaged with mediation first. By the time they reach a trial, the relationship — and often the asset pool — is in much worse shape than it needed to be."

— Dan Toombs, Founder & Accredited Mediator, Mediations Australia

Frequently asked questions

Can I get legal aid for family court in Australia?

Legal aid is available but means-tested and merit-tested. Eligibility thresholds are low — most people with a modest income and any assets do not qualify. Legal Aid offices also have limited capacity, and representation is often restricted to children's matters. Most property disputes proceed without legal aid.

What if we settle before trial — will I still pay all those costs?

No. Costs are proportional to the stage at which you settle. If you settle after filing but before any hearing, your combined costs may be $15,000–$40,000 rather than $100,000+. This is why settling early — ideally through mediation before filing — makes such a significant financial difference.

Can the court order my ex to pay my legal costs?

In limited circumstances. Family law courts generally make costs orders against a party whose conduct was unreasonable — for example, repeatedly ignoring subpoenas, making false allegations, or rejecting a reasonable offer that the court later betters. These orders are not automatic and are the exception rather than the rule.

Is mediation legally binding?

A mediated agreement itself is not automatically binding — but it can be made binding in two ways: by filing it with the court as consent orders (which the court approves and issues), or by having solicitors draft it as a Binding Financial Agreement under the Family Law Act. Either approach gives you the certainty of a binding document with the efficiency of mediation.

Does mediation work if my ex won't cooperate?

Mediation requires both parties to attend and engage. If your ex refuses, you may need to obtain a Section 60I certificate (which confirms genuine attempts at FDR were made) before the court will hear your application. We can advise on this and on situations where mediation is not appropriate — including cases involving family violence or where one party refuses to disclose financial information honestly.
