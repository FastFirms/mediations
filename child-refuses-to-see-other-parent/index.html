Child refusing to see a parent without court

When a child refuses to go to their other parent, both parents can feel helpless. This guide explains why it happens, what Australian family law expects of each parent, and the calm, practical steps that usually get things back on track without court.

Key takeaway: If your child refuses to see their other parent, do not ignore it and do not force it. Find out why, keep the other parent informed, and agree a plan together, ideally through family mediation. Under parenting orders, a child's refusal alone is not usually a reasonable excuse for not complying, so act early and get advice, unless there is a genuine safety risk.

Why children refuse to see a parent

It is one of the most distressing moments after separation: handover day arrives and your child says they will not go. Sometimes it is tears at the door; sometimes it is a teenager who simply stops answering the other parent's calls. Before deciding what to do, it helps to understand that refusal is almost always a signal, not a final decision. The common reasons are ordinary and fixable more often than parents expect.

  • Practical friction. A long drive, missing friends or sport on weekends, no space of their own, or a household routine that feels unfamiliar.
  • Loyalty conflict. Children who sense that one parent is hurt or angry may refuse the other parent to protect them. This is very common and often unspoken.
  • A new partner or blended family. Resentment of a new partner, step-siblings or a move to a new home.
  • Age and independence. Teenagers naturally want more say over their weekends, and rigid schedules made when they were younger can stop fitting.
  • A specific incident. An argument, a harsh punishment, or feeling unheard by the other parent.
  • Genuine fear or harm. In some cases the refusal reflects family violence, abuse, or exposure to conflict. This must be taken seriously and treated differently (see the safety section below).

Research from the Australian Institute of Family Studies has consistently found that it is ongoing conflict between parents, not separation itself, that does children the most harm. How the adults respond to refusal matters more than the refusal itself.

What the law says about a child refusing contact

Under the Family Law Act 1975, the child's best interests are the paramount consideration in every parenting decision. Since the May 2024 reforms, the court weighs a simplified set of best-interests factors, which include the child's safety, their views, their developmental and emotional needs, and the benefit of a relationship with both parents where it is safe.

A child's views are therefore relevant, but they are not decisive. There is no fixed age at which a child can choose which parent to see. The weight given to their views depends on their age, maturity and the reasons behind them. A considered view from a mature 15-year-old will carry far more weight than a reluctant 7-year-old who would rather stay home with their games console. We explain this in more detail in what age a child can choose which parent to live with.

If there are parenting orders in place, both parents must comply with them. The parent the child lives with is generally expected to take reasonable, positive steps to encourage the child to go, not simply leave the decision to the child. A child's refusal on its own is not usually accepted as a reasonable excuse for breaching an order, although a genuine and reasonable belief that the child would be at risk can be.

What to do in the first few weeks

The early response sets the tone. Children notice whether their refusal causes a crisis, and parents who stay calm and consistent tend to see the problem settle faster.

  1. Stay calm with your child. Listen without interrogating. Ask open questions such as "what's the hardest part about going to Dad's?" rather than "did something happen?"
  2. Encourage the time, positively. Speak well of the other parent, help pack the bag, and make clear that you want them to have a good time. Avoid offering competing treats on handover days.
  3. Tell the other parent promptly and neutrally. A short, factual message is best: what happened, what your child said, and that you would like to work on it together. Do not use the refusal as leverage.
  4. Keep a simple record. Dates, what was said, and steps you took. This protects both parents if things escalate.
  5. Try small adjustments. Shorter visits, a different handover location, a daytime activity instead of an overnight, or the other parent attending a sports game.
  6. Do not let it drift. A few missed weekends can quickly become months of estrangement that is far harder to repair.

If you are the parent being refused

Being rejected by your own child is painful, and the instinct to push back hard is understandable. The most effective responses, though, are patient and child-focused.

  • Do not blame the child. Keep calls, messages and birthday cards going, without pressure or guilt.
  • Look honestly at your side. Is there something about time at your home that could change, such as more one-on-one time, letting them bring a friend, or less screen-time conflict?
  • Avoid assuming alienation. True parental alienation does happen, but most refusal has more ordinary causes. Leading with accusations tends to entrench conflict.
  • Propose mediation early. A family dispute resolution practitioner can bring both parents together, and in child-inclusive mediation a qualified consultant can hear the child's views safely, without placing them in the middle.

You can find information about family dispute resolution and services near you through Family Relationships Online: Family mediation and FDR. If refusal persists despite reasonable efforts and there are orders in place, see our guide on what to do when an ex is not following parenting orders.

How mediation helps when a child refuses

Court can make orders, but it cannot make a reluctant child want to visit. Litigation over refusal often takes many months, involves family reports and lawyers on both sides, and can deepen the very conflict that sits behind the refusal. That is why court has its place, but it is rarely the best place to start.

Mediation works differently. A neutral, accredited mediator helps both parents:

  • share what each has seen and heard, without blame;
  • agree a gradual return plan, for example daytime visits first, then a single overnight, then the usual schedule;
  • adjust the schedule to fit the child's age, school and activities;
  • agree how both parents will talk to the child about the plan, using the same message;
  • decide whether counselling or a family therapist would help; and
  • record the agreement in an updated parenting plan or, if appropriate, consent orders.

Before most parenting applications can be filed, parents must attempt family dispute resolution and obtain a section 60I certificate, unless an exemption applies (FCFCOA family dispute resolution). Starting with mediation is therefore usually required anyway, and it is often where the problem is actually solved.

When refusal is about safety

If your child's refusal reflects fear, disclosures of harm, or exposure to family violence, the priority is protecting the child, not maintaining the schedule. In an emergency, call 000. For confidential support, contact 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732. Children and young people can call Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800.

Mediation may not be appropriate where there is family violence or a risk to a child, and exemptions from family dispute resolution apply. Get urgent legal advice about protecting the child and, if there are orders, about your options. The court's approach to risk is explained in FCFCOA: Safety and risk in children's matters, and our guide to domestic violence and family law sets out your options.

Rebuilding the relationship long term

Most refusal is temporary when both parents respond consistently. The families who come through it best tend to share a few habits: they keep the child out of adult disputes, they review arrangements as the child grows, and they get help early rather than waiting for positions to harden.

Consider building a regular review into your parenting plan, for example at the start of each school year, so the arrangement keeps pace with your child. If communication between you is difficult, our guides to co-parenting mediation and high-conflict mediation explain approaches that reduce friction, including shuttle mediation where you do not need to be in the same room.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

Can a child refuse to see a parent in Australia?+

There is no age at which a child can legally decide. A child's views are one of the best-interests factors, and their weight increases with age and maturity, but parents remain responsible for complying with any parenting orders.

Am I in breach of parenting orders if my child refuses to go?+

Possibly. The parent the child lives with is generally expected to take reasonable, positive steps to encourage the child to attend. A child's refusal alone is not usually a reasonable excuse, although a genuine, reasonable belief that the child is at risk can be. Get legal advice if refusal continues.

Should I force my child to see their other parent?+

Physically forcing a child is rarely helpful. Encourage the time positively, find out what is behind the refusal, and agree adjustments with the other parent, ideally through mediation. If there is a safety concern, protect the child first.

Can a mediator speak to my child?+

In child-inclusive mediation, a qualified child consultant can meet the child separately and share their views with the parents in a safe, age-appropriate way, so the child is heard without being put in the middle.

What if my child refuses because of family violence?+

Safety comes first. Call 000 in an emergency or 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732 for support. Mediation may not be appropriate and exemptions from family dispute resolution apply. Seek urgent legal advice.
