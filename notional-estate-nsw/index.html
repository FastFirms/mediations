Notional estate in NSW

In NSW, a family provision claim can reach property that never passed through the will. Here is what notional estate is, what it can catch, and how families resolve these claims without a long hearing.

Key takeaway: In NSW, a court hearing a family provision claim can treat property that never passed through the will, such as a jointly owned home, superannuation paid directly to a nominee, or assets given away shortly before death, as part of the estate. This is called notional estate. It exists only in NSW, and claims must generally be made within 12 months of death.

What is notional estate?

When someone dies, most people assume the "estate" is whatever the will deals with: the house in their sole name, bank accounts, shares. In practice, a large share of many people's wealth never passes through the will at all. A home owned as joint tenants passes automatically to the surviving owner. Superannuation is paid by the fund trustee, often straight to a nominated beneficiary. Assets may have been gifted or transferred to a family trust before death.

In most Australian states, those assets are simply out of reach of a family provision claim. New South Wales is different. Under Chapter 3 of the Succession Act 2006 (NSW), the Supreme Court can make an order designating property outside the estate as "notional estate", so it can be used to make provision for an eligible person who has been left without adequate provision. NSW is the only Australian jurisdiction with a regime this broad.

The look-back periods for gifts and transfers

Lifetime transactions are not caught indefinitely. Broadly, a transaction can be treated as notional estate if it:

  • took effect within three years before death and was entered into with the intention of denying or limiting provision for a person who could make a family provision claim;
  • took effect within one year before death at a time when the deceased had a moral obligation to provide for an eligible person that was substantially greater than any obligation to the person who received the property; or
  • takes effect on or after death, which is how jointly held property, superannuation and life insurance are usually caught.

Separately, a family provision claim in NSW must generally be filed within 12 months of the date of death. The court can allow a late claim if there is sufficient cause, but you should not rely on that.

The court's limits and the risk of costs

Notional estate is a last resort. Before designating property, the court must be satisfied that the actual estate is not enough to make the provision it considers appropriate, and it must weigh fairness to the people who hold the property, including the importance of not interfering with reasonable expectations about property and the substantial justice of the case.

That matters in practice because notional estate arguments make litigation longer, more complex and more expensive. Costs in NSW family provision cases are often ordered to come out of the estate, which means a contested hearing can shrink the very pool everyone is arguing about. The Supreme Court of NSW requires family provision matters to go to mediation before a final hearing in most cases.

How mediation helps with notional estate claims

Notional estate disputes often set a surviving spouse, who received the home by joint tenancy, against adult children from an earlier relationship. Emotions run high and every option the court has is blunt. Mediation gives the family room to agree something more precise.

  • Everything can go on the table. The parties can agree on outcomes involving jointly held property, super or trust assets without waiting for the court to decide whether to designate them.
  • Creative terms. A life interest in the home, staged payments, or a sale with a split of proceeds can all be agreed, where a court would usually order a lump sum.
  • Costs stay contained. Settling early preserves more of the estate for the family.
  • Relationships survive. A confidential conversation is gentler on grieving families than a public hearing.

What to do next

If you are thinking of making a claim: diarise the 12-month deadline from the date of death, gather what you know about jointly held property, super and gifts, and get advice from an estates lawyer early.

If you hold property that may be designated: keep records of how and when you acquired it, and what you contributed. Don't sell or transfer it without advice once a claim is in prospect.

Executors: you will usually need to hold off distributing the estate while a claim is possible. See our guide to executor disputes.

Questions & answers

Frequently asked questions

What is notional estate in NSW?+

Notional estate is property outside a deceased person's estate, such as jointly held property, superannuation paid to a nominee, or certain gifts made before death, that the NSW Supreme Court can treat as part of the estate in order to make provision for someone in a family provision claim.

Does notional estate apply in other states?+

No. Notional estate is unique to NSW. Other states have much narrower rules, so in most cases only property that passes under the will or intestacy is available for a family provision claim.

Can jointly owned property be claimed after death?+

In NSW, the deceased's interest in property held as joint tenants can be designated as notional estate, even though it passed automatically to the surviving owner. The court decides whether this is appropriate in each case.

Is superannuation part of notional estate?+

Superannuation paid directly to a nominated beneficiary does not form part of the actual estate, but in NSW it can be designated as notional estate in a family provision claim.

How long do I have to make a family provision claim in NSW?+

Generally 12 months from the date of death. The court can extend the time if there is sufficient cause, but late claims are not guaranteed.

Do notional estate claims go to mediation?+

Most family provision matters in the NSW Supreme Court are referred to mediation before a final hearing, and many settle there. Private mediation can also happen earlier, before proceedings are filed.
