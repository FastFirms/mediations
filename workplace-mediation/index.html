Workplace mediation

Workplace mediation for Australian organisations

Workplace mediation is a structured conversation in which an independent mediator helps people explore a workplace dispute and possible agreed ways forward. It is different from an investigation or a legal ruling. It starts with a suitability discussion, including safety, the people involved and any formal complaint or legal process already underway.

What is workplace mediation?

Workplace mediation brings the people in a dispute together, with an independent mediator, to discuss what has happened and what would make working together workable again. It is voluntary, and the participants shape any agreement.

It helps to be precise about what it is not:

  • Not an investigation. An investigator gathers evidence and makes findings of fact. A mediator does not.
  • Not a legal ruling. The mediator is not a judge, arbitrator or tribunal, and does not decide legal rights.
  • Not performance management or counselling. Those processes address one person's conduct or wellbeing, not a dispute between people.

The Fair Work Commission and the Fair Work Ombudsman have their own roles in workplace disputes; see the Fair Work Commission for official information. Mediations Australia is a private provider and is not part of the Fair Work system.

Which disputes fit

Mediation tends to suit disputes where the people involved need to keep working together, or need to agree how they will part ways, and both are willing to take part.

  • Manager–employee disputes. Disagreements about expectations, workload, feedback or how someone has been treated. The power imbalance matters: separate intake helps the mediator understand each person's position and whether a joint session is appropriate, and the organisation needs to have authority to implement any practical changes agreed.

  • Co-worker disputes. Communication breakdowns, overlapping roles, handover problems and working-style friction that has become personal. Mediation focuses on behaviour and practical arrangements, not on diagnosing personalities.

  • Leadership and executive conflict. Disputes within a leadership team or between a senior leader and a board or CEO.

  • Team conflict. Disputes involving several people. These are usually approached as facilitated multi-party work and quoted on request; see team conflict facilitation.

  • Restoring working relationships. For example after a restructure, a return from leave, or after an investigation has concluded but the relationships have not recovered.

  • Exits. Where an employment relationship is ending and the people involved want to agree terms. Independent legal advice is important here. See employment dispute mediation.

Suitability, and when another process comes first

Mediation is not right for every workplace dispute. Before anything is booked, the mediator considers:

  • Safety. Where there are allegations of violence, sexual harassment, threats or serious bullying, safety and any legal obligations come first. Mediation must never be used to suppress a complaint.
  • Whether facts need to be established. If serious allegations need findings, an investigation usually comes first. Mediation can help rebuild working relationships afterwards.
  • Deadlines and formal processes. Some claims have short statutory time limits. Arranging private mediation does not stop those clocks; check deadlines and get advice promptly. See the workplace dispute guide.
  • Willingness and capacity. Each person must agree to take part and be able to participate safely and genuinely.

Our suitability guide walks through these questions in more detail.

How the process works

  1. Referral. An employer (usually HR, a manager, the CEO or the board), the parties jointly, or one party can make contact. We clarify who is commissioning the mediation and who will receive what information before anything starts.

  2. Separate intake. The mediator speaks with each participant separately to understand their perspective, their goals and any concerns about safety or participation. Intake with each party is included in the fee.

  3. Participation and support. Whether a support person or representative attends is discussed at intake and agreed in advance.

  4. The mediation session. Usually a joint session, in person or online. Where direct contact is not appropriate, the mediator can work with each person separately (shuttle mediation).

  5. Agreed actions. If the participants reach agreement, the practical commitments are written down in plain language.

  6. Review. Where useful, a later check-in confirms whether the arrangements are working.

What the employer receives, and what stays private

Confidentiality is what allows people to speak candidly, but it is not absolute and it should never be assumed. Before intake, the commissioning organisation and the participants agree in writing:

  • what, if anything, will be reported back to the employer (often only whether the mediation went ahead and any agreed actions the participants consent to share);
  • how notes and records are handled;
  • the limits of confidentiality, for example where there is a risk to someone's safety or a legal obligation to disclose.

Whether discussions in mediation can later be used in legal proceedings depends on the law and the circumstances, so get legal advice if that matters to you. We do not promise blanket legal privilege.

Funding and independence

Most workplace mediations are paid for by the employer. Paying does not buy an outcome: the mediator is independent of all participants, including the organisation that pays, and does not take instructions about what the result should be. This is explained to every participant at intake.

Mediation and the Fair Work Commission

The Fair Work Commission deals with certain formal claims, such as unfair dismissal and general protections disputes, and has its own conciliation processes. Private mediation is different: it is arranged by agreement, usually earlier, and is not a substitute for the Commission where a formal claim is needed.

If a claim deadline may apply, get advice straight away. See the workplace dispute guide →

Questions & answers

Workplace Mediation — frequently asked

What is workplace mediation?+

A voluntary process in which an independent mediator helps people in a workplace dispute discuss the issues and work towards their own agreement. The mediator does not investigate, decide who is right or make a legal ruling.

Who can arrange workplace mediation?+

Usually an employer, through HR, a manager, the CEO or the board. Participants can also arrange it jointly, or one party can make contact and we will ask the other whether they are willing to take part. Everyone must agree to participate.

Does the employer paying affect neutrality?+

No. Most workplace mediations are paid for by the employer, but the mediator is independent of everyone, including the organisation that pays, and does not take instructions about the outcome. This is explained to each participant at intake.

Can a support person attend?+

Often, yes. Whether a support person or representative attends, and what their role is, is discussed at intake and agreed before the session so everyone knows who will be present.

What is reported to the employer?+

Only what is agreed in writing before intake. Often that is whether the mediation went ahead and any agreed actions the participants consent to share. Confidentiality has limits, such as safety risks or legal obligations, which are explained in advance.

When is an investigation needed instead?+

Where serious allegations need findings of fact, or there are safety concerns or legal obligations to respond, an investigation or other formal process usually comes first. Mediation can help rebuild working relationships afterwards.

How much does workplace mediation cost?+

Mediations Australia charges $2,200 + GST ($2,420) for a half-day workplace mediation or $3,800 + GST ($4,180) for a full day. The fee covers two parties and includes an intake session with each party and the mediation session. Each extra party is $500 + GST; travel is quoted separately.

Are the prices per person?+

No. Standard fees are totals for the engagement and cover two parties. Each additional party is $500 + GST ($550).
