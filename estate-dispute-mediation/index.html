What is estate dispute mediation?

Estate dispute mediation applies the same principles as family law mediation to disputes that arise after someone has died: contested wills, family provision claims, disputes between executors and beneficiaries, claims of undue influence, and arguments about how an estate is being administered.

The mediator is neutral, the process is confidential, and the outcome is shaped by the parties — not imposed by a judge.

What we resolve

  • Contested wills — challenges to validity, capacity, undue influence
  • Family provision claims — where someone feels inadequately provided for
  • Disputes between executors and beneficiaries — administration, valuations, distributions
  • Multi-beneficiary disagreements — about specific assets, sentimental items, or timing
  • Claims of unfulfilled promises — "Dad always said the farm would be mine"
  • Disputes about a deceased's earlier gifts or transfers
  • Conflicts in blended families — between a spouse and adult children from previous relationships

The legal landscape

Estate law varies between Australian states and territories, but the broad framework is similar everywhere: there's a will (or the rules of intestacy), an executor administering it, and various rights of beneficiaries and potential claimants. Disputes get pulled into state supreme courts — expensive, slow, and devastating to families. Courts increasingly direct parties to mediate before allowing matters to proceed. We make that step productive.

How the process works

  1. Initial consultation — understanding the dispute, who's involved, what the estate looks like.
  2. Engagement with the other parties — we reach out, explain the process, confirm participation.
  3. Pre-mediation intake — separate sessions with each party.
  4. The session — typically a full day, working through the issues with the mediator's guidance.
  5. Deed of settlement — the resolution is documented in a binding settlement deed.
  6. Implementation — the executor implements the agreed outcome.

When mediation helps

  • Before any court proceedings — saving everyone the cost
  • After a claim is filed but before it gets to a defended hearing — settle on the steps of court
  • Where the dispute is partly about money and partly about old family wounds — court can only do the money
  • Where there are multiple parties — mediation handles complexity better than litigation
  • Where the estate's value would be substantially eroded by legal fees

Estate litigation is unusually destructive

Estate disputes are particularly bad in court — they involve grief, old family dynamics, and a fixed pie that gets smaller with every legal fee. Mediation contains all of those costs.

Mediation vs court for estate disputes

Compared with estate litigation:

  • Cost — a fraction. Estate litigation regularly consumes 20–40% of the estate's value.
  • Time — weeks not years.
  • Privacy — the family's affairs stay private rather than entering the public record.
  • Relationships — mediation keeps families speaking; litigation rarely does.
  • Flexibility — mediation can craft creative solutions a court can't order (in-kind transfers, staged distributions).

Costs and timeframes

Estate mediation is typically a single full-day session with intake. Costs depend on complexity but are usually a single-figure thousands rather than the tens or hundreds of thousands estate litigation can run. The full process from consultation to settlement deed typically takes 4–8 weeks.

Getting started

The first step is a fixed-fee consultation. We'll listen to the dispute, assess suitability, and outline a process. Even where court proceedings have begun, mediation can usually still resolve the matter — often more quickly than the court process can complete.

Questions & answers

Estate Dispute Mediation — frequently asked

What is estate dispute mediation?+

A confidential, structured process where a neutral mediator helps parties resolve disputes about a deceased estate — contested wills, family provision claims, executor disputes — without going to court.

What kinds of estate disputes can be mediated?+

Contested wills, family provision claims, disputes between executors and beneficiaries, multi-beneficiary disagreements, claims of unfulfilled promises, and conflicts in blended families.

Is estate mediation legally binding?+

The mediation itself doesn't impose anything, but the agreement reached is documented in a binding settlement deed that's legally enforceable like any contract.

Can we mediate after court proceedings have started?+

Yes — estate matters frequently settle through mediation after proceedings are filed. Courts often direct parties to mediate before allowing the matter to proceed to a hearing.

Who pays for estate mediation?+

Often the estate itself pays, with the cost coming from the assets before distribution. Other arrangements are possible depending on circumstances.

How long does estate mediation take?+

Most matters resolve in a single full-day session, with the full process from consultation to settlement deed typically taking 4–8 weeks.

Can mediation work for complex blended-family disputes?+

Yes — it's particularly suited to multi-party situations that court would handle clumsily. The flexible format scales to the number of interests involved.
